Ten steps, from one month before to the day before the first class

The timings are only a guide. If the semester starts in two weeks, do steps 1, 2, 3, 7 and 10 first.

  1. One month before · Read the syllabus, then read the site as a student would

    Read the course card, which reproduces the official syllabus. Then read the calendar, the How to learn this course page and the whole of chapter 1. The lessons, the tutorials (TD) with solutions and the mini-project subjects are already written: your role is to guide students through this material, not to rewrite it all.

  2. Four weeks before · Install the tools on your computer

    On Windows, download the file run.bat from the course repository, put it in an empty folder and double-click it. It downloads the project, installs Python and the course libraries, then opens the site. The first time, you need an Internet connection: expect several hundred MB and 5 to 20 minutes. If Windows shows "Windows protected your PC", choose More info, then Run anyway.

    To check the installation, type cmd in the address bar of File Explorer while you are in the folder that contains run.bat, then type run.bat check: the list of installed libraries appears. run.bat notebook opens Jupyter. If the installation fails, use Google Colab: a Google account and a connection are enough. Start your self-study this week.

  3. Three and a half weeks before · Visit the computer room

    With the technical manager, check how many computers work, whether Python is installed, whether you may install libraries, and whether the Internet and the projector work. If Python can be installed, ask for it before the semester starts. If only the Internet works, students will work in Colab. If neither is available, copy the project folder to a USB stick and open docs/index.html in a browser: the lessons, solutions and interactive demonstrations work offline, and calculations are done by hand, as in tutorials (TD).

  4. Three weeks before · Create the class space

    Use your university's Moodle platform if it has one: it keeps a record of submissions. Otherwise, a Google Classroom class or a messaging group is enough. Post the site link, the calendar and the course rules there (late work, submissions, use of AI tools). Use one single channel, so that students know where to look.

  5. Two and a half weeks before · Prepare a diagnostic questionnaire

    About ten questions, online or on paper at the first session:

    • Do you have a personal computer? Regular Internet access?
    • Have you ever written a program, even a short one? Do you use a spreadsheet?
    • Do you read technical English easily, with a dictionary, or with difficulty?
    • Have you ever used an AI assistant? What for?
    • What do you expect from this course? What worries you?

    The answers help you form mixed pairs.

  6. Two weeks before · Plan the semester

    Copy the week-by-week plan onto your faculty's official calendar (holidays, public holidays, exams) and set the dates that students must keep: mini-project, oral presentations, round tables.

  7. Ten days before · Prepare the first session

    Start from the first of the lesson plans and test the projector in the room. Prepare an opening question ("Can a machine read a novel?") and the demonstration on the site's home page, which computes the sentiment of a sentence: it starts a discussion about what "reading" means for a machine.

  8. One week before · Set the assessment according to the regulations in force

    For assessment, the syllabus only refers to "the regulations in force". Ask your head of department how the marks for this teaching unit (UE) are split between the exam and continuous assessment, and what continuous assessment can include: tutorials (TD), mini-project, round table. Also settle a question that the syllabus leaves open: the teaching method asks for "at least 3 of the 5 proposed subjects", but the presentation of the mini-project says "Work on 1 subject of your choice". Announce a clear rule at the first session (see Assessment).

  9. Five days before · Prepare the mini-project brief

    You will hand it out at the end of chapter 2, but prepare it now: number of subjects, individual or pair work, deliverables (notebook, report of 4 to 6 pages, oral presentation), dates, review grid. For each subject, check that an accessible data source exists, or plan a small dataset to give to students.

  10. The day before · Final check, and your own ongoing training

    Check that the site opens on the classroom computer and that the questionnaire is ready. Then set aside two hours a week to prepare the following week: read the lesson, work on the tutorial (TD) without the solution, run the code of the lab session (TP). Staying one week ahead is enough. If a computer science colleague is available, plan a short meeting with them every two weeks.

If you have never programmed: two weeks to learn the basics

This course does not ask you to become an engineer. It asks you to read, run and adapt short programs and, above all, to help your students interpret the results. On this last point, your literary training is an asset. Asking a research question, criticising a corpus, distrusting a number without context: this is your profession.

The plan fits into ten one-hour sessions, five per week, in Jupyter or in Colab. Retype the examples instead of copying them, and predict the result before you run the code: this is the method recommended to students, and it works for you too.

When you get stuck

After twenty minutes on the same problem, stop. Write down what you have tried, then ask a colleague, or ask an AI assistant to explain the code line by line and check what it says (using an assistant well). Your students will get stuck in the same places, and you will know how to help them.

What the course really requires of you

  • Read a short program and explain what it does.
  • Run it, change a parameter, interpret the change.
  • Work through by hand: forward chaining, one step of gradient descent, one iteration of k-means.
  • Know where to find the answer: lesson, solution, documentation.

You do not need to write a long program from memory or to master the mathematics of neural networks.

A large part of the course can be done on paper: forward chaining, regression on three points, k-means with tokens on a grid, a Q-learning update. Reading a printed program and predicting its output is already programming.

Keeping to the plan, and adapting it

Following the plan group by group

The My groups page turns this table into a schedule for each of your groups: “Generate the schedule” creates the thirteen sessions from the date of the first one, with each week's topic and slides. If a session is lost, “Shift” moves all the following ones at once. After each session, note what was done, the homework set and the roll call: this is your class log.

If you fall behind

Falling behind is normal in the first year. Cut, in this order:

  • object-oriented programming: ten minutes of guided reading, since none of the mini-project code needs it;
  • the other algorithms (SVM, random forests, PCA): for reading, not for class time;
  • the details of CNNs and RNNs: keep the idea (images, sequences), and make exercise 4b independent work;
  • round tables 3 and 4, merged into one session;
  • some of the TD corrections: students can read some solutions at home, and class time goes to the exercises they find hardest.

Do not cut ethics, forward chaining, the supervised / unsupervised distinction or the time for the mini-project: they carry the target knowledge.

If the group moves much faster

  • Add backward chaining on the rules of exercise 1 of TD 3.
  • Have students compare two methods on the same data: TextBlob, VADER and a manual annotation for subject 1; two values of k for subject 2.
  • Offer real survey data (the World Values Survey in the references) or the subject 5 network in Gephi.
  • If the rule is one subject only, allow the fastest students to do a second one.
  • Give the most advanced students a tutor role in the lab sessions (TP): explaining forces you to understand.

If the group moves much more slowly

  • Move from writing code to reading code: predict the output of a program that works, then change one line.
  • Give programs with gaps: three lines to complete rather than a blank page.
  • Always follow the same order: calculation by hand, then demonstration, then code, as the site's method recommends.
  • Limit the tools to the libraries of the chosen subject; remind students that twenty minutes of coding a day is better than one long session.

A mixed-level group

In a literature master's programme, expect a few students who have programmed before and a majority who never have.

  • Form pairs based on the diagnostic questionnaire: one confident student and one beginner. The beginner uses the keyboard and the other guides; they swap every fifteen minutes.
  • Give each lab session (TP) a common core and an extension for the fastest students.
  • At the mini-project oral, ask each member to explain a notebook cell that you choose: this way, one student cannot write all the code alone.
  • Value interpretation as much as code.

Using the site in class

  • Project the demonstrations and ask students to predict before you click: the rule triggered in the next cycle of forward chaining, the effect of a step that is too large on gradient descent, the k-means assignments before "Next step". Then draw new starting centres: the result can change.
  • Ask students to skim the lesson before class and bring two questions, as planned in the students' typical week. Start the session with these questions rather than with a full lecture.
  • Do the quizzes by a show of hands, then reveal the explanation; in tutorials (TD), require twenty minutes of work before anyone opens a solution.
  • Use the FR / EN / عربي selector and the glossary to fix the vocabulary in several languages.

Key points

  • Keep the fixed points of the syllabus: 1, 2 and 6 weeks, and the mini-project handed out at the end of chapter 2.
  • If you fall behind, cut the extra material, never ethics or the mini-project.
  • Stay one week ahead of your students: that is enough.

The diagnosis and the response

You are right about one thing: in an hour and a half a week, language students will not learn to write programs. But they can still learn a great deal. A student who reads ten lines of Python, predicts their output, runs them, changes a value and explains why the result changed has understood what the machine does. The syllabus asks for the "ability to use the Python software", not for the skills of a professional developer. And the hardest step, saying what a number means for a text or for a class, is already part of their training.

What they will not do

  • Write a program on a blank page.
  • Learn syntax by heart.
  • Be graded on handwritten code.

What they will do

  • Run a complete notebook, cell by cell.
  • Predict in writing what a cell will display.
  • Change a single value and observe the effect.
  • Interpret the result in their discipline, and write down its limits.

Two tried and tested approaches

The PRIMM approach (Predict, Run, Investigate, Modify, Make; prédire, exécuter, examiner, modifier, créer) was described by Sue Sentance, Jane Waite and Maria Kallia (Computer Science Education, 2019), based on work in English secondary schools. Pupils predict the output of a working program while discussing it with a classmate, run it to check, investigate it, modify it, and only write their own program at the end. The Use–Modify–Create progression of Irene Lee and her co-authors (ACM Inroads, 2011) follows the same logic.

Both approaches were designed for young pupils learning to program, not for master's students in languages: we borrow the order of their steps, without promising results. And we stop before their last step. In your classes, "create" means producing an analysis (choosing data, asking a question, interpreting, writing), not a program.

What changes in assessment

You grade understanding and interpretation, never the code: a reasoned prediction, even a wrong one, the explanation of a line, a comment on a result. In the mini-project grid, "Method and code" becomes: the notebook runs, the changed values are justified, and the student explains orally a cell that you choose (details).

The ground rule

Never a blank page of code. Each cell is read, predicted and run; only one value changes at a time; each session ends with a few lines of interpretation written in the student's discipline. The grade is based on these lines, not on the code.

ANG · working in English

Notebooks and the English version of the site, VADER, spaCy's English model. Written answers are given in English: each notebook becomes practice in the language and its technical vocabulary.

For linguists: AntConc and learner corpora, preferably your own students' work, anonymised and with their consent; access to published corpora may require a licence.

LGC · literary corpora

Notebooks in French, public-domain texts (Gallica, Wikisource, Gutenberg). The comparative approach fits well: the same analysis on a text and its translation, on two authors, on two periods.

The question to keep asking: what does calculation see that close reading does not, and the other way round?

DID · the language classroom

Notebooks in the language the students will teach, French or English. Notebook 09 is designed for them: readability, lexical profile, gap-fill text, a learner's answer compared with the expected answer.

A topic of their own: AI tutors in the language classroom. Have students test a chatbot as a corrector or as a conversation partner, then note its successes, its errors, the rules it invents and what happens to learners' data (assignments that keep their meaning).

Running a notebook in Colab for the first time

  1. A Google account

    Each student uses their own. Have students create one before the session: in class, this takes time and often requires a phone.

  2. Open the link

    "Colab (FR)" or "Colab (EN)" opens the notebook in the browser. The first time you run it, Colab warns that the notebook was not written by Google: choose "Run anyway".

  3. Run

    Cell by cell, with the ▶ button or Shift + Enter, after reading the prediction question. To run everything again: "Runtime › Run all".

  4. Change only the "TO CHANGE" fields

    These are boxes, sliders or lists on the right of the cell. After a change, run this cell and the following ones again. Do not touch the rest of the code.

  5. Save a copy

    "File › Save a copy in Drive"; the copy goes into the "Colab Notebooks" folder. Without it, everything is lost when the notebook is closed.

  6. Hand in the work

    "File › Download › Download .ipynb", or "File › Print" then "Save as PDF". The file keeps the outputs and the written answers.

The 90-minute session, step by step

  1. Opening question · 5 min

    A question from their discipline: "Can a machine tell whether a review of a novel is positive?"

    You: write the answers on the board without correcting them; you will come back to them.

  2. Predict on paper · 15 min

    A printed worksheet: the main cell of the notebook and three questions (what will it display? which word will count most? what if we change this number?). In pairs, in writing, with screens off.

    You: read the code aloud, saying what each line does; collect a few predictions.

  3. Run · 15 min

    The notebook is run from top to bottom; at each cell, students compare the output with their prediction.

    You: walk around the room without taking the keyboard. When a student says "it doesn't work", first answer: "Read the last line of the message." Project your notebook for anyone who has lost track.

  4. Change a field · 20 min

    One value at a time: the sentence, the number of groups, the text. A new prediction, then a new run.

    You: insist on a prediction before each attempt; spot two surprising results to show everyone.

  5. Interpret · 15 min

    In pairs, then as a class: what does this result mean for a student of English, a comparatist, a future teacher? What does the method not see?

    You: go back to the answers from the start, link them to the lesson, and ask for an example from the output for each claim.

  6. Write, save, sign out · 20 min

    In the last text cell, 100 to 150 words: what I predicted, what I got, what I conclude for my discipline, one limitation. In English for the ANG groups. Then a copy in Drive, submission, sign-out.

    You: read two or three paragraphs over students' shoulders; record the session in "My groups".

Three exam questions on printed code

Each question gives a short program and its real output (Python 3.11, scikit-learn 1.9): students read, recover an erased line, explain and interpret, without writing code. Suggested marking scheme: 6 + 7 + 7. For the ANG groups, translate the questions and the examples, then run the code again: the outputs change with the text.

Question 1 · Counting words (6 points)

  1. What does the line that starts with mots = do? Describe its three operations in one sentence each. (1.5 points)
  2. Why does the program display 13, then 8? Name the two concepts. (1.5 points)
  3. Write the erased last line. (1.5 points)
  4. Without .lower(), the third line displays 9: why? A student concludes that "the" is the most important word in the text: what do you answer? (1.5 points)
Show the solution and marking scheme

1. lower() converts the text to lower case; the two replace calls remove commas and full stops; split() cuts the text at the spaces and produces a list of words. 0.5 per operation.

2. 13 words counted with their repetitions, 8 distinct forms: the, sea, is, calm, sky, grey, and, silent; in corpus linguistics, tokens (occurrences) and types (formes). Give credit for the ratio 8 / 13, about 0.62, which notebook 09 calculates.

3. [('the', 3), ('is', 3), ('sea', 2)]. 1 point for the three words and their counts, 0.5 for the format. In case of a tie, Counter keeps the order of first appearance: accept ('is', 3) before ('the', 3).

4. Without lower case, "The" and "the" are two different strings, so two forms: 9 (0.5); the last line becomes [('is', 3), ('sea', 2), ('the', 2)]. "The" is a function word, the most frequent word in almost any English text: frequency is not importance. You remove stop words, compare with a reference corpus (TF-IDF) and go back to the text (1).

Question 2 · A sentiment score (7 points)

  1. Explain in two sentences what the score function does. (1 point)
  2. The second sentence gets −1. Why? Is this the reader's opinion? (1.5 points)
  3. In the fourth sentence, "long" is counted but "réussie" is not. Why? What would need to change, without writing any code? (1.5 points)
  4. What knowledge does the program lack for the third sentence? (1 point)
  5. Would you use this program to analyse 300 reader reviews or learner comments? What would you do before trusting the scores? (2 points)
Show the solution and marking scheme

1. It splits the sentence into words (in lower case, without punctuation), adds 1 for each positive word, subtracts 1 for each negative word, and returns the total.

2. Only "ennuyeux" is recognised; the negation "n'est pas" is ignored, because each word is judged on its own (1). The opinion is rather favourable (0.5). The sentiment reader on the site's home page, inspired by VADER, handles negation.

3. The program compares exact strings: "réussie" is not "réussi" (0.5). You would need to add the inflected forms, or reduce each word to its lemma with spaCy (0.5). And "long" is not always negative ("un long poème"): the list ignores context (0.5).

4. Irony: "Magnifique, vraiment" followed by a criticism. The program would need to grasp tone and intention; it only sees one positive word.

5. Not as it is. Annotate a sample by hand (50 reviews, for example), compare it with the scores, classify the errors (negation, irony, inflection, missing words), enrich the lists or change the method, and present the result with this margin of error. For learner comments: anonymity and consent. 1 point for checking on a sample, 1 for a justified limitation or precaution.

Question 3 · Learner profiles (7 points)

  1. Supervised or unsupervised learning? Justify your answer from the code. (1.5 points)
  2. What does n_clusters=2 mean? Who chose this value? (1 point)
  3. Using the last output, describe the two groups in words. Do the numbers 0 and 1 have a meaning? (2 points)
  4. A colleague concludes that reading in English reduces speaking anxiety. What do you think? (1.5 points)
  5. Give one ethical precaution for using such a classification in a real class. (1 point)
Show the solution and marking scheme

1. Unsupervised (0.5): there are no labels and no right answers; fit_predict receives only the answers and forms the groups itself, using k-means (1).

2. The number of groups requested (0.5). The analyst set it, not the machine; the elbow method in subject 2 helps to choose it (0.5).

3. Each row of cluster_centers_ is an average profile. Group 1, the first four learners: 1.5 hours of reading a week, anxiety 4.5, that is, "reads little, very anxious when speaking". Group 0, the last four: 6.5 hours, anxiety 1.5, that is, "reads a lot, not very anxious" (1.5). The numbers are arbitrary and may be swapped depending on the version of scikit-learn; naming the groups is the researcher's job (0.5).

4. This is not supported: fictitious data, eight people, and clustering describes similarities without establishing a cause (0.75). The link could go the other way (less anxious learners read more) or come from a third factor, such as the level in English (0.75).

5. One justified precaution is enough: an anonymous questionnaire answered with consent; no "anxious" label in a file or in front of the class; no decision about a learner based on two variables; profiles presented as hypotheses for discussion.

Key points

  • Grade the reasoned prediction, the explanation and the interpretation, never the syntax.
  • An exam can show code and its output: students read it, complete an erased line, explain and criticise.
  • Every output printed in an exam paper must have been obtained by running the code.

The human neuron

Objective
Show that an artificial neuron calculates a weighted sum, and that learning means correcting the weights after an error.
Duration
30 min
Materials
Three "0 / 1" cards, a sheet of weights written in pencil, the board.

Three students are the inputs: x₁ is 1 if the text is in verse, x₂ if it rhymes, x₃ if it contains dialogue. The neuron calculates z = w₁ · x₁ + w₂ · x₂ + w₃ · x₃ + b and answers "poem" if z > 0, "prose" otherwise. Start: w = (0, 1, 1), b = −0.5. If the neuron wrongly says "poem", subtract 1 from the bias and from the weights of the inputs that are 1; if it wrongly says "prose", add 1.

  1. A sonnet by Du Bellay · 5 min

    (1, 1, 0), z = 0.5: "poem". Correct.

  2. A passage of dialogue from Bel-Ami · 5 min

    (0, 0, 1), z = 0.5: "poem". Wrong. New weights: (0, 1, 0), b = −1.5.

  3. Whitman's "Song of Myself" · 5 min

    Unrhymed free verse, (1, 0, 0), z = −1.5: "prose". Wrong. New weights: (1, 1, 0), b = −0.5.

  4. Check · 5 min

    The three texts and a descriptive paragraph without dialogue are correctly classified.

  5. Borderline cases · 10 min

    Racine's Phèdre (1, 1, 1) comes out as "poem"; a poem from Le Spleen de Paris (0, 0, 0) comes out as "prose". Who is right?

What students should take away

Learning means adjusting numbers after errors: the weight for dialogue fell to zero because this clue was not useful. The neuron does not see what its inputs do not describe, and the categories remain human choices.

Objective
Let students feel that gradient descent only knows the local slope.
Duration
25 min
Materials
Eleven sheets numbered from 0 to 10, laid in a line on the floor, with the altitude written on the back.
Altitude hidden on the back of each sheet

Altitude97545642136

The hiker only turns over their own sheet and the two next to it, then takes one step towards the lower of the two if it is lower than their own; otherwise, they stop.

  1. Metaphor · 3 min

    The position is the parameter; the altitude is the error.

  2. Start at 0 · 5 min

    0, 1, 2, 3: the hiker stops at altitude 4.

  3. Start at 10 · 4 min

    10, 9, 8: the hiker stops at altitude 1.

  4. Lifting the fog · 3 min

    All the sheets turned over: a local dip at 3, the minimum at 8.

  5. Big steps · 5 min

    Steps of four sheets from 0: 0, 4, 8; the big step jumps over the dip at 3 and finds the minimum. Steps of three sheets from 10: 10, 7, stop at altitude 2, right next to the minimum.   6. Formula · 5 min

    w ← w − η · slope, where η is the length of the step; the lesson's demonstration with η = 0.1, then 1.1.

What students should take away

The algorithm only sees the local slope. The starting point can trap it in a dip; a step that is too large makes it oscillate or diverge. The function in the course has only one valley; those of neural networks are much more rugged.

The imitation game

Objective
Let students live through Turing's test (1950) and discuss what it measures (history, Chinese room).
Duration
25 to 40 min
Materials
Strips of paper, a place out of sight, the rule card below; optionally, a chatbot.

  1. The original game · 5 min

    In Turing's version, an interrogator must guess in writing who is the man and who is the woman; then a machine takes one of the places.

  2. Roles · 3 min

    X answers sincerely, Y plays the machine using the card; both are out of sight. You read the answers aloud: handwriting would give the witnesses away.

  3. Question · 10 min

    Five minutes of written questions per witness, the length of time Turing mentions.

  4. Vote · 5 min

    A show of hands, then the reveal.

  5. Discuss · 10 min or more

    What gave the machine away? Should it imitate our mistakes? In Turing's example, it answers an addition after about thirty seconds, and gets it wrong. Is deceiving the same as thinking?

Rule card for the "machine", in the spirit of ELIZA

  • The question contains "you": "Why are you asking me that question?"
  • It contains a word about family: "Tell me about your family."
  • It asks for a calculation: wait thirty seconds, then answer.
  • Otherwise: "I am not sure I understand. Could you rephrase that?"

Variation: Y is a chatbot whose answers you read out; the question becomes "what does deceiving prove?".

What students should take away

Turing replaces a philosophical question with an observable test. Passing the test does not prove understanding (Searle). We easily attribute intelligence to anything that talks: this is the ELIZA effect.

A spam filter based on word votes

Objective
Help students understand the training, the predictions and the errors of a text classifier, without probabilities.
Duration
30 min
Materials
The ten messages below, printed; the board for the count.

Spams: "Gagnez un voyage gratuit, cliquez ici"; "Offre exceptionnelle : cliquez vite pour gagner un cadeau"; "Vous avez gagné un cadeau gratuit, cliquez ici". Non-spams: "Le cours de jeudi porte sur le chapitre 3"; "Merci de rendre le mini-projet jeudi"; "Voici le lien vers le corrigé du TD".

Stop words are ignored (le, la, les, un, une, de, du, des, à, au, pour, vous, votre, est, et, sur, en, ce). A word's vote is the number of spams that contain it minus the number of non-spams that contain it. A message's score is the sum of the votes of its words, with an unknown word counting as 0: positive means spam; negative, non-spam; zero, undecided.

  1. Train · 10 min

    Expected: cliquez +3; gratuit, ici, cadeau +2; gagnez, gagner, gagné, offre, exceptionnelle, vite, voyage, avez +1; jeudi −2; the other words of the non-spams −1.

  2. Predict · 10 min

    "Cliquez ici pour gagner un livre gratuit": +8, spam. "Le TD de jeudi est reporté": −3, non-spam. "Cliquez ici pour lire le corrigé du TD de jeudi": +1, wrongly marked as spam. "Félicitations, vous êtes sélectionné": 0, undecided.

  3. Discuss · 10 min

    The third message is a false positive; the fourth has only unknown words. "Gagnez", "gagner" and "gagné" vote separately (hence lemmatisation), and "jeudi" has become a clue by chance.

What students should take away

A classifier turns words into numbers and learns only from its examples; its two kinds of error are measured by precision and recall. The naive Bayes classifier in the lesson does the same thing with probabilities.

Tips for leading activities

Group work

Three or four students, with mixed levels and equipment, rotating roles (secretary, spokesperson, timekeeper) and a precise output. Draw the spokesperson by lot: everyone must be ready. Ask "what have you tried?" rather than giving the answer.

Pair programming

The driver holds the keyboard; the navigator reads the task, checks the code and makes suggestions. They swap every fifteen minutes, at your signal. The less confident student starts at the keyboard, and the navigator never takes it from them.

Live coding

A large font, a notebook prepared with the headings, and code typed live while you say what you are doing. Have students predict each result. Make mistakes on purpose: an unclosed quotation mark (SyntaxError), a badly indented block (IndentationError), an unknown variable (NameError), "Nedjma" + 1956 (TypeError). Always read the last line of the message. If a real error defeats you, say so and come back to it next time.

Students without a computer

Identify them through the questionnaire. Put them in pairs, book the computer room if possible, and prepare tasks on paper: predict the output of printed code, find the error in it. A phone is enough to read the site, but hardly enough to program. Assessment must not depend on personal equipment.

The languages of the class

Let groups discuss in the language in which they think fastest, but require the technical terms in French and in English. The site also exists in French and in Arabic: a student who is stuck can reread the lesson in another language.

Exercise 1 · Course questions (4 points)

  1. Give a definition of artificial intelligence in two or three sentences. (1 point)
  2. What distinguishes weak (or narrow) AI from strong (or general) AI? Give two real examples of weak AI. Does strong AI exist today? (1.5 points)
  3. For each situation, say whether it is supervised or unsupervised learning, and justify your answer in one sentence. (1.5 points)
    • a) We have 400 poems whose period (Romanticism, Parnassianism, Symbolism) has been identified by specialists; we want to assign a period to 2,000 undated poems.
    • b) We want to sort the 300 answers to a survey on reading habits into groups, with no categories fixed in advance.
    • c) Using 1,000 press articles whose year of publication is known, we want to estimate the year of undated articles.
Show the solution and marking scheme

1. Definition (1 point). Computing methods that enable a machine to perform tasks associated with human intelligence: recognizing, classifying, predicting, reasoning, producing language. 0.5 for the "intelligent" tasks, 0.5 for the idea of methods. Accept the definition based on agents (perceiving an environment and acting to achieve a goal).

2. Weak AI and strong AI (1.5 points). 0.5: weak or narrow AI performs a limited task and cannot transfer its skill; strong or general AI would be capable of any human intellectual task, or even, in Searle's sense, would really have a mind. 0.5: two real examples, 0.25 each (machine translation, spam filter, character recognition, Deep Blue, AlphaGo, book recommendation). 0.5: no, no strong AI is recognized; discussing the debate on large language models and concluding that there is no consensus earns full marks. Give credit for, but do not require, the distinction made in the course between weak/strong (philosophical) and narrow/general (technical), which the syllabus confuses.

3. Situations (1.5 points: 0.25 for the type and 0.25 for the justification, per situation). a) Supervised: the 400 dated poems are labeled examples; this is classification. b) Unsupervised: no category is given, and the algorithm forms the groups itself (clustering). c) Supervised: the known year of the 1,000 articles serves as the label; since the answer is a number, this is regression. A common trap in c): answering "unsupervised" because the articles to be dated have no label. But that label is exactly what the model predicts.

Exercise 3 · Gradient descent (3 points)

We are looking for the minimum of the error function J(w) = (w − 2)² + 1. You may take as given that its derivative is J′(w) = 2(w − 2). At each step, gradient descent applies:

wₖ₊₁ = wₖ − η · J′(wₖ)

  1. With w₀ = 6 and η = 0.25, calculate w₁ and w₂, as well as J(w₀), J(w₁) and J(w₂). (1.5 points)
  2. For which value of w is the error smallest, and what is the value of J there? What is the derivative at this point? (0.5 point)
  3. Take two steps again from w₀ = 6, this time with η = 1. What do you notice? What is the role of η? (1 point)
Show the solution and marking scheme
Two steps of gradient descent with η = 0.25
kwₖJ(wₖ)J′(wₖ)wₖ₊₁ = wₖ − 0.25 × J′(wₖ)
06(6 − 2)² + 1 = 172 × 4 = 86 − 2 = 4
14(4 − 2)² + 1 = 52 × 2 = 44 − 1 = 3
23(3 − 2)² + 1 = 22 × 1 = 2not asked (2.5)

1. (1.5 points) w₁ = 4 and w₂ = 3 (0.5 each); J(w₀) = 17, J(w₁) = 5, J(w₂) = 2 (0.5 for all three). The derivative is positive because we start to the right of the minimum: w decreases and the error goes down at each step.

2. (0.5 point) The minimum is at w = 2, where J′(2) = 0: the slope is zero at the bottom of the valley. There, J equals 1, not 0: the "+ 1" raises the curve without moving its minimum.

3. (1 point) J′(6) = 8, so w₁ = 6 − 8 = −2; J′(−2) = 2 × (−4) = −8, so w₂ = −2 + 8 = 6 (0.5). We are back at the starting point: w oscillates endlessly between 6 and −2, and J stays at 17. The learning rate η sets the length of the step; if it is too large, the step jumps over the minimum (0.5). Remark, not required: wₖ₊₁ − 2 = (1 − 2η)(wₖ − 2), so the distance to the minimum is halved with η = 0.25 (4, 2, 1) and only changes sign with η = 1.

Continuous assessment in a few minutes

These checks show where the class stands before it is too late, with almost no marking. If your faculty has a continuous assessment mark, they can count toward it under the conditions it sets.

The five-minute quiz

At the start of the session, ask three questions on the previous session, on half a sheet of paper. Students swap sheets and mark them while you give the answers on the board.

Example after chapter 1: 1) Who proposed the "imitation game" in 1950? 2) Is a spam filter narrow or general AI? 3) Grouping 200 survey answers with no categories: supervised or unsupervised?

The exit ticket

Three minutes before the end, each student writes one thing they understood and one question they still have, and answers one targeted question. Read the tickets in ten minutes; open the next session with the most common difficulty.

Example after gradient descent: "If the derivative is negative, does w increase or decrease? Why?"

Mini-exercises in lab sessions (TP)

Three quick formats: predict the output before running the code, fix code that contains an error, change a line to get a different result.

Example: titres = ["Nedjma", "Al-Ayyam", "Bel-Ami"], then titres.append("Frankenstein"), then print(len(titres), titres[-1]). Answer: 4 Frankenstein. For a fixing exercise, write df["Livres"] instead of df["livres"] and ask students to read the last line of the KeyError message.

Peer review

Two students swap the outline of their mini-project (research question, planned data) or the draft of their report. Each one fills in the self-assessment grid for the other and notes two strengths and two questions.

If you mark the exercise, mark the quality of the review, never the work reviewed.

Key points

  • Marking arrangements depend on the regulations in force: find out what they are, then announce them in writing.
  • Assess understanding: reading, predicting, calculating by hand, interpreting.
  • Marking schemes and grids are suggestions; hand out the grids together with the assignment.
  • If you change any data in an exercise, you must redo the whole solution.

A usage charter for the class

A total ban cannot be enforced; allowing everything empties assignments of their meaning. So hand out a charter at the very first session. This one follows the four rules of the lesson Outlook, limits and ethics (personal data, copyright, transparency, verification) and the advice on the Study method page. Your faculty's own rules, if there are any, take precedence. The round table The outlook for AI, which ends with a charter, lets you amend it with the class.

Preamble, to read to the students

In this course, an AI tool can help you learn; it cannot learn for you. Your marks measure what you have understood. You must be able to explain everything you hand in, orally, without the tool.

What is allowed

  • Asking for an explanation of a course concept, an extra example, a simpler rewording.
  • Getting the tool to quiz you and to correct your own answers.
  • Getting an error message or a piece of code explained, line by line.
  • Getting help to fix your own code, provided you can explain the fix.
  • Translating documentation written in English, in order to understand it.
  • Getting the spelling of your own text corrected, and declaring it.

What is forbidden

  • Using an AI tool during an exam, a quiz or written work in class, unless told otherwise.
  • Handing in a generated text, code or analysis as your own, even if reworded.
  • Citing a reference, quotation, date or figure provided by a tool without checking it in the source.
  • Uploading personal data (survey answers, private messages) or unpublished or protected texts to an online service.
  • Reading out a text produced by a tool during a round table or an oral defence.
  • Failing to declare a use.

How to declare use

Every piece of work handed in (mini-project report, round table summary) ends with a "declaration of use of AI tools", even when the answer is "none". It includes:

Tool

Sample declaration

"I used a chatbot (free online version, November 2025) to understand a pandas error message and to translate two paragraphs of the scikit-learn documentation. My requests are in the appendix. I checked the fix by running the whole notebook again. The choice of corpus, the analysis, the interpretation and the writing are my own."

If a rule is broken

Start with a conversation: ask the student to explain their work. Proven cheating falls under the institution's rules. In Algeria, ministerial order no. 933 of 28 July 2016 sets the rules for preventing and combating plagiarism. Check the applicable regulations with your department before you announce a sanction.

Assignments that keep their meaning

Assume that any text written at home may have been produced by a tool. Rather than trying to detect it, move the evidence of learning toward what the tool cannot do in the student's place: explaining orally, showing a process, working on their own data, commenting on their own results.

Detectors are not reliable

Software that claims to spot generated text is often wrong. OpenAI withdrew its own detector in 2023 because it was not accurate enough. A study by Weixin Liang and co-authors (Patterns, 2023) showed that these tools often wrongly label as "generated" texts written in English by people whose native language is not English. A detector score proves nothing; a conversation with the student about their work tells you more.

Five levers

  • The oral defence of the mini-project, already part of the deliverables, is the strongest lever. Ask questions that only the author can answer: "Show the cell that cleans the data and explain each line." "Change k = 3 to k = 4 in front of me: what changes?" "Read a text from group 2: why is it there?"
  • The logbook: one dated entry per work session, in three lines (what I tried, what failed, what I decided and why). Example: "14 November. VADER rates ironic messages in darja written in Latin script as positive. I am reading 30 messages by hand to measure the problem." Mark regularity and clear thinking, not style.
  • The personal corpus: each student or group works on data they have gathered themselves. For example: the editorials of an Algerian daily newspaper over one week, the poems of an author studied in another course of the master's, or the anonymous answers to a questionnaire given, with consent, to their own year group. A tool can help with the code, but it does not know this corpus, and interpreting it requires having read it.
  • Work in class: calculations by hand in tutorials (TD), "predict before you run" in lab sessions (TP), fifteen minutes of writing on a course question. Homework becomes practice, and cheating becomes less worthwhile.
  • Questions about their own results: "Quote three messages that your model misclassified and explain each error." "Your group 3 brings together two texts that seem unrelated: what do they share in the data?"

An exercise that uses the tool instead of avoiding it

Ask a chatbot yourself to explain a course concept, for example non-deducible facts. In class, students look for the errors, inaccuracies and unverifiable claims in its answer, then correct them using the course. They learn the concept and also how to read these tools critically. Read the answer first, so that you know what they should find.

Using an assistant to prepare your classes

An AI assistant saves time, especially when the technical part of a course is unfamiliar to you. The rule is the same as for your students: it proposes, you check, and you remain responsible for what you hand out.

Exercise variants

For a resit, or to give different papers to different groups, ask for variants of a tutorial (TD) exercise. Redo each solution yourself: calculation errors are common.

Quiz questions

An assistant quickly produces multiple-choice questions with plausible distractors. Check that only one answer is correct and that the vocabulary is the course's own.

Translations

Instructions, glossary, summaries: a first draft in Arabic or English is quicker to review than to write. Check the technical terms against the course glossary and, for Arabic, with a colleague: terminology varies from one country to another.

Explanations for you

Before a session, have a concept or a piece of code explained to you. Ask the questions your students will ask, then compare the answers with the lesson on the website.

Sample request

"Here is a k-means exercise from my course: [paste the exercise]. Suggest three variants of the same difficulty, with six whole-number values, k = 2 and two starting centers. No value may be at an equal distance from the two centers, at any step. Give the detailed solution for each variant, iteration by iteration."

The request specifies the difficulty, the form of the data and the trap to avoid. The assistant can still make mistakes: check.

Before you hand out a document prepared with an assistant

  1. Redo the calculations

    Do them without looking at the proposed solution, then compare. For k-means, check each distance; for chaining, check each cycle.

  2. Run the code

    Run it in Jupyter or Google Colab, and copy the output you actually get, never the output the assistant announces.

  3. Check the facts

    Check dates, names, quotations and references in the lessons on the website, a library catalog or the original document: assistants invent plausible references.

  4. Align with the course

    Use the same notation (∧, η, μ₁), the same terms and the same level as the tutorials (TD): an unfamiliar notation confuses students on exam day.

Precautions

  • No personal student data, no unpublished text. Never upload names, marks, exam scripts, emails or unpublished theses to an online service. In Algeria, Law no. 18-07 of 10 June 2018 governs the processing of personal data. To have an exam paper reviewed, send only the paper.
  • No delegated marking. The mark is your responsibility. The European AI Act also classifies systems that assess students as "high-risk".
  • The service settings. Check whether your conversations can be used to train the model, and whether you can turn this option off.
  • Transparency. Tell your students when a document was prepared with an assistant and then checked: you show them the kind of use the charter asks of them.

Key points

  • A clear charter, handed out at the first session and discussed in a round table, is better than a ban that cannot be enforced.
  • Move the evidence of learning toward oral work, the process, the personal corpus and work in class; detectors prove nothing.
  • For your preparation: the assistant proposes; you redo the calculations, run the code and check the facts.
  • No personal student data in an online service.

Resources in Arabic and for Arabic

Most of the tools in this course were designed for English. The following resources deal with these difficulties. The UNESCO texts on the Ethics shelf also exist in Arabic, for example the Recommandation sur l'éthique de l'intelligence artificielle.

Before students collect data themselves

Questionnaires, online comments, messages: these are personal data under Algerian Law 18-07 (Ethics shelf). Inform the people concerned, keep only what is necessary, and anonymise before analysis. Also, a text that is online is not necessarily free of copyright: prefer the public domain and open licences, and always make students cite the source.
