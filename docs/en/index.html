Four habits that work

Rereading your notes and highlighting feel productive, but little of it stays. A review of research on learning (Dunlosky et al., 2013) ranks two techniques among the most useful instead: testing yourself and spacing out your revision. Add explaining ideas out loud, and trying exercises before looking at the solution.

Test yourself instead of rereading

Close the page and write down what you remember: a definition, the steps of k-means, the difference between forward and backward chaining. Then check. The "Self-check" quizzes on this site are made for this.

Space out your revision

Review a lesson the next day, three days later, a week later, then three weeks later. Four short sessions beat one long session the night before the exam.

Explain it simply

Explain an idea in a few sentences to someone who does not take the course, or in writing. Where you get stuck shows you what to reread.

Try before the solution

Work on each tutorial exercise for at least twenty minutes before opening "Show the solution". A mistake you find and fix yourself is remembered far better than a solution you read.

Testing yourself forces you to pull the information out of memory, and that effort is what makes it stick. Rereading and copying take almost no memory effort.

Ideas and landmarks. Make your own timeline (ten dates are enough) and, for each pair of notions (weak and strong AI, supervised and unsupervised learning), a table of differences written from memory. These tables are exactly what the exam and the round tables ask for.

Chapter 2

A skill, learned by doing. Twenty to thirty minutes of code almost every day beat one long session. Do not copy and paste: retype. Before running code, predict what it will print. Read error messages to the end: the last line almost always says what is wrong. To practise, run.bat notebook opens Jupyter, and Google Colab works with nothing to install.

Chapter 3

Algorithms. For each one, follow the same order: compute it by hand on a tiny example (the tutorial exercises), then use the interactive demo on this site, then write a few lines of scikit-learn. If you can redo the calculation by hand, you have understood the algorithm.

Mini-project

Choose your subject as soon as chapter 2 ends, and start small: a corpus of fifty texts that works is better than ten thousand that do not. Keep a logbook (what you tried, what failed, why). Show your progress to your teacher every week.

Prepare three arguments, one precise example and one checked source. Listen to the others so you can answer their arguments, not only make yours.

Learning with an AI assistant, without cheating

A chatbot can be a good tutor, as long as you ask it to make you work rather than to work in your place.

Uses that help

  • "Ask me five questions about forward chaining and correct my answers."
  • "Explain gradient descent with an example about reading."
  • "Here is my reasoning for exercise 2: where is the mistake?"
  • Asking for a line-by-line explanation of code you did not understand.

Uses that harm

  • Copying code you could not explain.
  • Asking for the solution of an exercise before trying it.
  • Quoting a number, date or reference without checking it: chatbots invent them.
  • Handing in generated text as if it were your own.

For the mini-project, declare any use of an AI tool, as the lesson on ethics explains.

What is artificial intelligence?

The term "artificial intelligence" first appeared in 1955, in a proposal written by John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester and Claude Shannon. They were asking for funding to bring researchers together at Dartmouth College, in the United States, in the summer of 1956. Their working hypothesis: every aspect of learning and intelligence could in principle be described precisely enough for a machine to simulate it. This workshop is considered the birth of the field.

Yet there is no single definition, because there is no agreement on what the word "intelligence" itself means.

AI as the design of agents

The standard textbook by Stuart Russell and Peter Norvig, Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach (1995), presents AI as the study of agents. An agent (agent) is a system that perceives its environment and acts on it to achieve a goal. A spam filter, for example, "perceives" the text of an email and "acts" by putting it in the right folder.

A set of techniques rather than a single thing

Talking about "AI" in the singular is misleading. AI is a family of very different techniques: logical rules written by experts, algorithms that explore possibilities, statistical methods that learn from examples, and artificial neural networks. So when you see a result produced by "an AI", always ask: which technique, trained on which data, for which task?

Working definition

We will call artificial intelligence (intelligence artificielle, IA) the set of computing methods that allow a machine to perform tasks that we usually associate with human intelligence: recognizing, classifying, predicting, reasoning, planning, and understanding or producing language. This definition is about observable tasks. It says nothing about what the machine "understands".

Why AI matters for literary studies

The term digital humanities (humanités numériques) refers to research in literature, history or the social sciences that relies on computer tools. Its origin is often dated to 1949, when the Italian Jesuit Roberto Busa began indexing the works of Thomas Aquinas, with help from IBM.

For a comparatist, the most stimulating proposal comes from Franco Moretti ("Conjectures on World Literature", 2000; Distant Reading, 2013). No reader can read every novel published in the 19th century, so he proposes distant reading (lecture distante): studying thousands of texts through measurable features, such as genres, titles, character networks or the movement of forms from one language to another. AI provides the tools for this large-scale reading, without replacing close reading.

Three uses in literature and the humanities

  • Corpus studies: identify the themes and entities (people, places, dates) that dominate a set of novels, press articles or political speeches.
  • Stylometry: measure style to discuss an attribution. In 2019, for example, Florian Cafiero and Jean-Baptiste Camps provided quantitative arguments in favor of Molière in the controversy that set him against Corneille.
  • Translation studies: align a novel by Naguib Mahfouz sentence by sentence with its French and English translations, to compare the translators' choices.

Historical overview of artificial intelligence

The history of AI alternates between enthusiasm and disappointment. It is also a contest between two families of ideas: the symbolic approach, which programs explicit rules and reasoning, and the connectionist approach, which trains artificial neural networks. The most cited founding text, Alan Turing's article, opens with this sentence:

I propose to consider the question, "Can machines think?"

Alan M. Turing, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence", Mind, 1950
  1. 1943

    The formal neuron

    Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts proposed a mathematical model of the neuron: a unit that adds up signals and "fires" when they pass a threshold. It is the ancestor of today's neural networks.

  2. 1950

    The imitation game

    Turing proposed a test: an interrogator has a written conversation with a human and with a machine. If the interrogator cannot tell them apart, the machine is behaving intelligently. This has been called the "Turing test" ever since.

  3. 1956

    The Dartmouth workshop

    The term "artificial intelligence" became established as the name of the field. At the workshop, Allen Newell and Herbert Simon presented the Logic Theorist, a program that proves theorems in logic.

  4. 1958

    The perceptron

    Frank Rosenblatt presented a neural network that adjusts its parameters from examples. In 1969, Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert (Perceptrons) showed its limits, which held back the connectionist approach for a long time.

  5. 1966

    ELIZA

    At MIT, Joseph Weizenbaum published a program that imitates a psychotherapist by rephrasing the user's sentences with simple rules. Yet some users believed it truly understood them: this is the "ELIZA effect".

  6. 1973

    The Lighthill report and the first winter

    In the United Kingdom, a report by the mathematician James Lighthill judged the results to be far below what had been promised. Funding fell, first in the United Kingdom and then in the United States. This was the first "AI winter", which lasted until around 1980.

  7. 1970s–1980s

    The age of expert systems

    At Stanford, MYCIN helped diagnose bacterial blood infections using a few hundred "if… then…" rules. In the 1980s, companies adopted these systems, and in 1982 Japan launched its "fifth-generation" computer program.

  8. Late 1980s

    The second winter

    Expert systems were expensive to maintain and fragile outside their domain, and they disappointed. The market for Lisp machines collapsed in 1987, and budgets shrank until the early 1990s.

  9. 1997

    Deep Blue beats Kasparov

    IBM's computer beat the world chess champion Garry Kasparov. Its strength came mainly from exploring millions of positions very quickly, rather than from learning.

  10. 2012

    AlexNet and the rise of deep learning

    In the ImageNet image recognition competition, the convolutional network AlexNet (Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, Geoffrey Hinton) clearly outperformed the other methods. Large annotated datasets, graphics processors and deep networks came together.

  11. 2016

    AlphaGo

    DeepMind's program beat Lee Sedol, one of the world's best go players, by four games to one. It combined neural networks, reinforcement learning and the exploration of a tree of moves.

  12. 2017

    The Transformer

    The article "Attention Is All You Need" (Vaswani et al., Google) introduced the Transformer architecture, based on the attention mechanism. It is the basis of almost all of today's large language models.

  13. 2018

    BERT and pre-trained models

    Google presented BERT, a model pre-trained on large amounts of text that can then be adapted to many tasks. The same year, OpenAI released its first GPT model.

  14. 2022

    ChatGPT

    On November 30, OpenAI opened ChatGPT to the public. It is a chatbot based on a large language model. So-called generative AI entered everyday use, including at university.

  15. 2024

    The European AI regulation

    The European Union adopted Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, known as the AI Act, which classifies systems according to their level of risk. It is being applied in stages, starting in 2025.

Cycles of hope and disappointment

Each technical wave brings predictions that are too optimistic. In 1958, Herbert Simon and Allen Newell announced that a computer would be world chess champion within ten years; it took nearly forty. But abandoned ideas do not disappear. Neural networks, judged to have no future in 1969, came back in 1986 with backpropagation (David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton, Ronald Williams), and then dominated the field after 2012. A lesson in method: judge a system on what it actually does, measured by tests, and not on what people promise it will do.

Narrow (weak) AI and general (strong) AI

The syllabus brings together two pairs of notions that are close but distinct. The contrast between weak and strong AI comes from the philosopher John Searle (1980). It separates a machine that simulates intelligence from a machine that would really possess a mind. The contrast between narrow and general AI is about the range of abilities: one specific task, or all human intellectual tasks. The first distinction is philosophical, the second is technical; everyday usage often mixes them up.

Narrow (or weak) AI

Narrow AI (IA étroite) performs a well-defined task, often better than a human, but cannot transfer this skill to anything else. The program that beats chess champions cannot read a poem.

  • Optical character recognition (OCR) on old newspapers.
  • Machine translation between Arabic, French and English.
  • Spam filters, book recommendations.

General (or strong) AI

Artificial general intelligence (IA générale, AGI) refers to a hypothetical system able to perform any human intellectual task. In particular, we would expect it to be able to:

  • transfer its knowledge from one domain to another;
  • learn a new task from very few examples;
  • in Searle's sense, truly understand what it processes.

The Chinese room argument

In "Minds, Brains, and Programs" (1980), Searle imagines a person who does not know Chinese, locked in a room with a manual written in their own language. The manual tells them which Chinese characters to send back in reply to the ones slipped in through a slot. From outside, the room seems to answer perfectly in Chinese, yet the person understands nothing. According to Searle, a program in the same way manipulates symbols according to their form (syntax) without reaching their meaning (semantics). So passing the Turing test would not prove understanding. His critics reply that it is the system as a whole that would understand. The debate remains open.

What about large language models?

Today's chatbots write, summarize and translate on almost any subject. Whether they are a step toward general AI is hotly debated. However, they are systems trained mainly on text, first to predict how a sequence of words continues, then adjusted to hold a dialogue. They make mistakes that a human would not make. Some researchers see emerging reasoning abilities in them; others, such as Emily Bender and her co-authors (2021), see "stochastic parrots" that recombine forms they have learned. There is no consensus that they are general AI. Treat them as powerful but specialized tools, and check everything they produce.

Supervised learning and unsupervised learning

Most of today's AI is based on machine learning (apprentissage automatique), a term attributed to Arthur Samuel (1959). Instead of writing all the rules of a task, we give examples to an algorithm, which fits a model: a mathematical function that can then handle new cases.

Supervised learning

In supervised learning (apprentissage supervisé), each example comes with the correct answer, called a label (étiquette). Suppose that specialists have assigned 600 poems to a period: Romanticism, Parnassianism or Symbolism. The model observes features of each poem (vocabulary, line length, punctuation) and learns to associate them with the given period. It can then suggest a period for an unknown poem. When the answer is a category, we speak of classification; when it is a number, such as a year of composition, we speak of regression.

Train, test, generalize

A model is never evaluated on the examples used to train it. The data are split in two. The training set (jeu d'entraînement) is used to fit the model. The test set (jeu de test) is set aside and used at the end to measure its performance. The goal is generalization: handling new cases well, like a student who passes an exam made of questions they have never seen.

The main trap is overfitting (sur-apprentissage). A model that is too complex "learns by heart" the training poems with all their accidental details, instead of grasping what characterizes each period. It gets almost perfect results in training and poor results in the test, like a student who memorized the solutions without understanding the course. A large gap between the two scores is the warning sign. Another trap: if all your Romantic poems come from old editions that write "enfans" for "enfants", the model will learn the spelling of the edition rather than the style of the period.

Unsupervised learning

In unsupervised learning (apprentissage non supervisé), the data have no labels. The algorithm looks for regularities by itself, most often by forming groups of similar items: this is clustering (partitionnement). Take 200 French-language novels from the Maghreb and measure their average sentence length, the richness of their vocabulary and the frequency of function words ("de", "que", "et"). Without knowing anything about the authors or the dates, an algorithm can sort the novels into stylistic families. Interpreting them is up to you: do these groups correspond to authors, generations, genres, or to nothing meaningful?

In between, and beyond

Labeling data takes a lot of time and expertise. Semi-supervised learning (apprentissage semi-supervisé) therefore combines a few labeled examples with many unlabeled ones. Large language models rely on a form of learning called self-supervised: they learn to guess a hidden word or the next word, and the text itself provides the "right answer". Finally, in reinforcement learning (apprentissage par renforcement), an agent acts, receives rewards or penalties, and learns by trial and error. It will be studied in chapter 3.

You have 500 political speeches that political scientists have sorted into three categories (economy, security, education). You want the machine to classify 3,000 new speeches. What type of learning is this?

This is supervised learning: the 500 classified speeches are the labeled data that train the model. It is normal that the new speeches have no label, because that is exactly what the model must predict. We would speak of unsupervised learning if no speech were labeled and we were looking for groups.

AI approaches to problem solving

To solve a problem, AI offers several strategies. They appeared at different moments in its history, and today's systems often combine them. Knowing how to tell them apart will help you choose a tool that suits your research question.

State-space search

The problem is described as a set of situations (the states) linked by actions, and we look for a path from the initial state to the goal state. Planning a route between Médéa and Algiers works this way, and so does a chess program. Algorithms such as A* (1968) guide the search. Limit: the number of states grows very quickly.

Symbolic reasoning

Knowledge is written as facts and "if… then…" rules. An inference engine deduces new facts from them (forward chaining) or works back from a goal to the facts (backward chaining). This is the principle of expert systems, studied in chapter 3. Strength: every conclusion can be explained. Limit: the rules take a long time to write.

Statistical learning

The model learns from numerical examples: regression, decision trees, probabilistic classifiers, clustering. These methods are available in scikit-learn. They work well for survey data and medium-sized corpora, as long as the measured variables are well chosen.

Connectionism

Artificial neural networks, organized in layers, learn by adjusting millions of parameters; when there are many layers, we speak of deep learning. This approach dominates vision, speech and text processing. But it needs a lot of data and computing power, and its decisions are hard to explain.

Agents and reinforcement

An agent acts in an environment, receives a reward or a penalty, and gradually learns the best behavior. This approach produced AlphaGo. It is used in robotics and helps adjust chatbots based on human judgments. It will be covered in detail in chapter 3.

Hybrid approaches

Several approaches are combined to add up their strengths. AlphaGo combines neural networks with search in a tree of moves. An assistant that consults a document database before answering combines information retrieval with a language model. We speak of neuro-symbolic AI when networks and logical rules work together.

One problem, two approaches: identifying the language of a message

You are studying 10,000 messages posted on Algerian social networks, in Standard Arabic, French, English or Darja (Algerian Arabic), which is sometimes written in Latin letters. First step: identify the language of each message.

With rules. If the message contains Arabic characters, it is Arabic. If it contains several of the words "le, la, les, et, est", it is French. If it contains "the, and, is, of", it is English. This approach is transparent and needs no prior data. But it fails on very short messages, on messages that mix languages, and on Darja written in Latin letters ("wach rak"), which no simple rule can capture.

With learning. You have a few thousand messages labeled by hand. A model learns how often sequences of two or three characters occur in each language, a method described as early as 1994 by William Cavnar and John Trenkle. You then evaluate it on a test set. It recognizes unexpected cases, including Darja in Latin letters if it appears in the examples. But it requires annotated data, and it reproduces your annotation choices: is a message that mixes French and Darja "French" or "Darja"? This scientific decision is yours.

In practice, the two are often combined: a rule for the script (Arabic or Latin), and a trained model for the rest.

Outlook: opportunities, limits and ethics

Opportunities for the humanities and social sciences

AI opens up fields of study that used to be out of reach for lack of time: exploring thousands of texts (digitized newspapers, archives, novels); transcribing printed or handwritten documents, including those in Arabic script; analyzing the open answers of a survey; measuring opinion on social networks; extracting the entities and themes of a political speech; rebuilding networks of correspondents or of characters. The mini-project topics draw on these uses. In every case, the tool produces clues; it is your research question that gives them meaning.

Limits you should know

  • Bias. A model reproduces the imbalances in its data. Languages that are rare on the web, such as Darja or Tamazight, are handled less well than English. For a long time, machine translation tools rendered a gender-neutral Turkish sentence, with no "he" or "she", as "he is a doctor" but "she is a nurse".
  • Hallucinations. Generative models produce plausible sentences, not truths. They can confidently invent quotations, dates and references.
  • Opacity. We cannot easily explain why a deep network produced a given result, and this is a problem for scientific argument.
  • Energy cost. Training and using large models consumes a lot of electricity, as well as water to cool data centers. A small, well-chosen model is often enough.

Invented references

Never copy a reference given by a chatbot until you have found it in a catalog, a database or the original document. In a thesis, a reference to a source that does not exist is a scholarly fault, whatever its origin.

Ethics and good practice

In November 2021, UNESCO's member states adopted the Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence, the first global standard-setting framework on the subject. It sets out principles such as proportionality, respect for privacy, transparency and explainability, responsibility, human oversight and fairness. The 2024 European regulation turns some of these principles into obligations that depend on the level of risk. Social scoring of citizens is banned; systems that admit or assess students are "high-risk"; chatbots must make clear that they are machines. For your own work, four rules apply:

  • Personal data. Survey answers and online messages concern real people: obtain informed consent, keep only what is useful, and anonymize. In Algeria, Law No. 18-07 of June 10, 2018 governs the processing of personal data; in Europe, the GDPR does.
  • Copyright. A protected text may be analyzed for research under the applicable rules, but it may not be freely distributed. Do not upload unpublished or protected texts to an online service that gives itself the right to reuse content.
  • Transparency. Declare any use of an AI tool in a thesis: its name, version and date, the precise use (translation, rephrasing, corpus analysis) and the instructions you gave. Since 2023, the APA and MLA styles have offered citation models for these tools. Presenting a generated text as your own is plagiarism.
  • Verification. Check every fact, quotation and figure produced by a tool. Keep a record of your processing steps so that a reader can repeat them.

Key points

  • AI is a family of techniques (rules, search, statistical learning, neural networks). It was officially born at Dartmouth in 1956.
  • Its history alternates between enthusiasm and "winters": judge a system on what it does, not on what people promise it will do.
  • All existing AI systems are narrow. It has not been established that general AI exists, including in the form of large language models.
  • Supervised learning starts from labeled examples and is evaluated on a test set. Unsupervised learning forms groups that you must interpret.
  • Bias, hallucinations, opacity and energy cost call for verification and transparency, and for respect for personal data and copyright.

For your thesis, a chatbot suggests a complete reference: author, title, publisher, year. What is good practice?

Language models can invent perfectly plausible references: a real author, a believable title, a well-known publisher. Only a check in an independent source can confirm that the reference exists and that it really says what is attributed to it.

Setting up the working environment

Python is a programming language: a vocabulary and a grammar for giving precise instructions to a computer. It is free and easy to read, and it is widely used in the digital humanities to build corpora, count words, and produce tables and charts. The code is run by an interpreter, which reads it line by line; a file of Python code (extension .py) is called a script. Use Python 3, version 3.11 or later: Python 2, which you will still find in old tutorials, has not been maintained since 2020.

Three ways to work

Google Colab

A notebook (notebook, the same word in French) mixes code cells, results and text. Colab offers notebooks in the browser: there is nothing to install, a Google account is enough, and pandas and NumPy are already there. Keep your files in Google Drive: the files stored in the session are deleted when it closes.

A distribution that installs Python, Jupyter Notebook and the main scientific libraries in one go. It works offline, but it takes up several gigabytes.

The official installer, which is lightweight. You then add each library with pip, the Python package manager, from the terminal.

UTF-8 encoding, a precondition for any work on texts

A computer stores only numbers. An encoding is the table that matches these numbers to characters. UTF-8, the universal encoding, covers French accents, the Arabic alphabet, Tifinagh and almost every writing system in the world. If a UTF-8 file is read with another encoding, "é" becomes "Ã©", Arabic becomes unreadable, or Python stops with a UnicodeDecodeError.

  • Always write encoding="utf-8" when you open a file: otherwise, on Windows, Python 3.11 uses the regional encoding, which cannot handle Arabic.
  • Save your texts in UTF-8 (the "Encoding" menu in the "Save As" window).
  • From Excel, choose the "CSV UTF-8" format. When Excel is set to French, it often separates columns with semicolons: in that case, read the file with pd.read_csv("fichier.csv", sep=";").

The fundamentals of the language

This review goes over the basic ideas one by one. Type the examples yourself: typing mistakes, and the error messages they cause, are part of learning.

Variables and types

A variable is a name that refers to a value in memory. The = sign means "receives", not "is equal to": annee = 1956 stores 1956 under the name annee. Each value has a type, which determines what you can do with it: whole number (int), decimal number (float, from floating point; in French virgule flottante), character string (str, from string; in French chaîne de caractères) or Boolean (bool), which is either True or False.

Division with / always produces a float (380.0), and a comparison such as annee < 1962 produces a Boolean. A variable name contains no spaces and, preferably, no accents: words are joined with an underscore (mots_par_page).

Strings

Each character in a string has a position, its index, which starts at 0; index -1 refers to the last character. A slice (tranche) such as vers[:10] extracts positions 0 to 9. Strings have methods, attached functions that you call with a dot: lower() converts to lowercase, replace(a, b) replaces a with b, and split() cuts at spaces and returns a list of words.

The last line is an f-string: in a string preceded by f, each expression in curly braces is replaced by its value. replace() does not change vers: it returns a new string.

Lists

A list is an ordered sequence of values that can be changed, written in square brackets. You can add items to it (append), sort it (sort), measure its length (len) and read its items by index or by slice.

Tuples

A tuple is written in parentheses and cannot change once it is created: it is immutable (immuable). This rigidity is intentional: a bibliographic reference (title, author, year) is a whole that no part of the program should change by mistake. Because it is immutable, a tuple can also be used as a dictionary key, which a list cannot.

The second line performs unpacking (déballage): the three values are stored in three variables in one go. The last line, disabled by #, shows the error that a change would produce.

Dictionaries

A dictionary links keys to values, just as a reading card links headings to their content. It is written in curly braces, with clé: valeur pairs, and you access it by key, not by position. Keys are unique.

items() gives the key-value pairs. get() returns a fallback value where fiche["genre"] would cause a KeyError.

Sets

A set (set) is a collection with no order and no duplicates: it gives the distinct values of a list (the vocabulary of a text, the languages of a corpus) and tests membership very quickly with in. Its display can vary from one run to the next, so sort it with sorted(). Be careful: {} creates an empty dictionary; an empty set is written set().

Conditions, loops and functions

A condition (if, elif, else) runs a block only if a test is true. A loop with for repeats a block for each item in a sequence; a while loop repeats it as long as a test stays true. A function, defined with def, groups instructions under a name: it receives parameters and returns a result with return. Blocks are marked by indentation: the lines indented by four spaces after the colon belong to the block.

The string in triple quotes under def is a docstring, which documents the function; // is integer division (1861 // 100 gives 18). Be careful with while: if the word you are looking for were missing, the index would go past the end of the list and Python would stop with an IndexError.

List comprehensions

A list comprehension (compréhension de liste) builds a list from another one in a single line: [expression for element in sequence if condition]. It reads like a sentence: "the list of m, for each m in mots, if m has at least six letters".

With mots = "la mer la mer toujours recommencée".split() (a line by Paul Valéry, without punctuation), what is the value of len(set(mots))?

The list contains six words, and "la" and "mer" each appear twice. The set keeps only one copy of each value (la, mer, toujours, recommencée): 4 distinct forms. len() works on a set just as it does on a list.

The NumPy, pandas and matplotlib libraries

A library (bibliothèque) is a set of functions written by other people, which you load with import. Three of them form the foundation of data analysis: NumPy for calculations, pandas for tables, matplotlib for charts. By convention, they are imported under a short alias: import numpy as np, import pandas as pd.

NumPy: calculating on a series of numbers

NumPy provides the array (array), a sequence of numbers on which an operation applies to all the items at once. This is called a vectorized operation: nb_mots / 250 divides the ten numbers without a loop.

The mean (118,000 words) is far above the median (68,500), which splits the series into two halves: a single very long novel, Les Misérables, pulls the mean up. The standard deviation, which measures the spread around the mean, is high for the same reason. When texts have very different lengths, always look at the median.

pandas: Series and DataFrame

A Series is a column of values with an index, the labels of the rows (0, 1, 2… by default). A DataFrame is a table made of several Series that share the same index: the equivalent of a spreadsheet, where each row is an observation (a work) and each column is a variable.

So that the example works without a file, we build the DataFrame from a dictionary: each key becomes a column name. The corpus brings together works in French, Arabic and English; the Arabic titles are transcribed in Latin letters (Al-Ayyam is translated into French as Le Livre des jours). The word counts are rounded values invented for the exercise, not real counts.

Reading a CSV file

In real work, the table comes from a CSV file (comma-separated values; in French valeurs séparées par des virgules): a text file where each line is a record and the columns are separated by commas. Let us save our table, then read it back with pd.read_csv().

Exploring: head, info, describe

head() shows the first rows, tail() the last ones and shape the dimensions. info() lists the columns, their type and the number of non-empty values, which reveals missing data; describe() summarizes the numeric columns.

With pandas 2, text columns appear as object instead of str and the first line is slightly different; the content is the same.

count is the number of values, mean the mean, std the standard deviation, and 25%, 50% and 75% the quartiles (50% is the median). The standard deviation from pandas (151,524) is different from the NumPy one (143,748): pandas divides by n − 1 and NumPy by n, unless you write nb_mots.std(ddof=1).

Selecting, filtering, sorting

A column name in square brackets returns a Series; a list of names, hence the double brackets, returns a DataFrame.

To filter, you write a condition on a column: it produces a Series of True and False, and df[condition] keeps the rows that are true. Conditions are combined with & (and) or | (or), each one in parentheses. The original index (5, 6, 7) is kept; tolist() converts a Series into a list.

sort_values() sorts the rows by a column; ascending=False reverses the order.

Grouping and counting

groupby() splits the rows into groups according to a column, then calculates on each group: "how many words on average for each genre?". value_counts() counts the occurrences of each value, from the most frequent to the rarest.

The genre "récit" is placed after "roman": Python sorts strings by the Unicode number of their characters, and "é" comes after all the letters without accents (lesson 5 fixes this trap). Besides, with only one récit in the corpus, the "mean" for this genre does not mean much: an exact calculation does not make a solid conclusion.

Adding a column

You create a column by assigning a calculation to a new name; the calculation applies to the whole column. round() rounds the values, and astype(int) converts them to integers.

A word about matplotlib

matplotlib draws charts, and pandas uses it directly: applied to a Series, plot(kind="bar") draws a bar chart.

This code draws three vertical bars, one per genre (poésie, roman, récit), whose height is the average number of words; the bar for the novel is clearly the tallest. The title and the axis labels are not decoration: without them, the chart cannot be interpreted. savefig() saves a PNG image that you can insert into a thesis.

Introduction to object-oriented programming

Until now, data and processing were kept apart. Object-oriented programming (programmation orientée objet) brings them together: an object groups data, its attributes, and the actions that apply to it, its methods. You already use objects: a DataFrame has the attribute shape and the method head().

Class, instance, attributes, methods

A class is a mold; each object made with this mold is an instance of the class. The Texte class describes what a text is for our program: a title, an author, some content, and three actions.

  • __init__ is the constructor: Python calls it automatically when each instance is created, to fill in its attributes.
  • self refers to the instance itself: self.titre means "the title of this particular text". It is the first parameter of every method, but you never pass it yourself: Python does it for you.
  • Counter, from the collections module, counts the items in a list; most_common(n) returns the n most frequent ones (see lesson 5).

You create an instance by calling the class like a function. The most frequent words are function words (la, je) and an isolated "l", which comes from the naive splitting of l'aube and l'heure: elision is a classic difficulty in the automatic processing of French.

Composition: a corpus made of texts

An object can contain other objects: a Corpus has a list of Texte instances. This is composition. The text knows how to count its words; the corpus adds up the words of its texts.

total_mots() delegates the counting to the nb_mots() method of each text. Walt Whitman's English poem (1865) is handled in the same way as the French poems.

Inheritance: a poem is a text

Inheritance defines one class from another. class Poeme(Texte) means that a poem is a particular kind of text: it receives the attributes and methods of Texte, its parent class, and adds what is specific to it, here counting the lines of verse.

Poeme defines neither __init__ nor nb_mots(): it uses those of Texte. isinstance() confirms that a poem is also a text.

Procedural programming

A sequence of instructions and functions that receive the data as parameters.

  • The data passes from function to function: compter_mots(texte).
  • Simple and direct for a short script.
  • Hard to organize as the project grows.

Object-oriented programming

Objects that carry their own data and methods.

  • The object acts on its own data: texte.nb_mots().
  • Suited to long-term projects and reusable tools.
  • More code to write at the start; this is how pandas and scikit-learn are built.

Reading and processing text files

A corpus usually comes as plain text files (.txt), with no formatting, which any program can read. We will work on Arthur Rimbaud's "Le Dormeur du val" (1870), a sonnet whose last line reveals that the sleeping soldier is dead.

Writing and reading a file

open() opens a file in a mode: "r" to read (the default), "w" to write (the existing content is erased), "a" to add at the end. The with block closes the file automatically, even if an error occurs. Triple quotes allow a string to run over several lines.

read() returns the whole content as one string; readlines() returns a list of lines, each one ending with the end-of-line character \n, which strip() removes. The 17 lines contain 14 lines of verse and 3 empty lines between the stanzas.

Going through a folder

The pathlib module represents paths as Path objects: / joins the parts of a path, glob("*.txt") finds the .txt files, and read_text() and write_text() read and write in one line. This program stores Rimbaud's poem and a famous line by al-Mutanabbi (10th century) in a folder named corpus, then makes a rough count of their words.

This count based on spaces is approximate, and in different ways depending on the language. In French, typography puts a space before ";" and ":", and split() counts these signs as words. In Arabic, the conjunction wa ("and") and the article al- are attached to the next word: والليل ("and the night") counts as a single word. To compare multilingual corpora, you must define what a "word" is before you count.

Searching

in answers true or false, find() gives the position of the first occurrence (-1 if it is absent), and count() counts the occurrences. All three are sensitive to case (capital and small letters): "Dort", at the start of a line, is found only after the text is converted to lowercase.

A regular expression (expression régulière, or regex), handled by the re module, is a pattern that describes a whole family of strings. In r"\w+ment\b", \w means a letter or a digit, + means "one or more times" and \b a word boundary; | means "or". The r before the quotation marks creates a raw string, which passes backslashes on unchanged.

The "ment" pattern finds the two adverbs in the poem, but it would also catch a noun such as moment. The second expression picks out the lexical field of sleep: five occurrences in fourteen lines, which prepare the final twist.

Counting frequencies

From a list, Counter builds a kind of dictionary that links each word to the number of times it appears. We distinguish tokens (occurrences), each appearance of a word, and types (formes), the different words.

Without a filter, the top places go to stop words (mots vides), the articles and pronouns found in every text. Once these are removed, dort comes first. Counting has its limits: nue ("tête nue") and nue ("sous la nue", under the clouds) are added together, while trou (first line) and trous (last line), which frame the sonnet, are counted separately. Grouping the forms of the same word is the job of lemmatization, which tools such as spaCy perform.

Sorting

sorted() returns a new sorted list. Its key parameter receives a function that calculates, for each item, the value used for sorting; reverse=True reverses the order. lambda creates a small function with no name: lambda paire: paire[1] receives a (word, frequency) pair and returns the frequency.

Sorting by frequency gives the same result as most_common(). The last example fixes the trap seen with pandas: Théophile Gautier's Émaux et Camées was placed after Nedjma. For a complete catalog, you remove all the accents in the key with the unicodedata module.

A KWIC concordance

A concordance shows each occurrence of a word with its left and right context, all aligned on the word searched for. This format is called KWIC (keyword in context; in French mot-clé en contexte): reading down the column reveals patterns that a linear reading misses.

The lines of verse are joined on one line and separated by "/", the usual convention for quoting several lines of verse in running text. re.finditer() gives the position of each match (start(), end()), and we cut thirty characters on each side of it. The rf prefix combines a raw string and an f-string; in {gauche:>{largeur}}, > aligns the left context to the right, which places the keywords in a column. We can thus see dort come back in each of the last three stanzas, like a refrain that prepares the final revelation.

Concordances, from the Bible to the digital humanities

The concordance existed long before computers: as early as the 13th century, handwritten concordances of the Latin Bible listed every passage in which a word appears. In 1949, the Italian Jesuit Roberto Busa began the Index Thomisticus with IBM, a computerized concordance of the works of Thomas Aquinas, which is often presented as the birth of the digital humanities. The KWIC format was made popular around 1960 by Hans Peter Luhn, an engineer at IBM.

Free software such as AntConc, TXM or Voyant Tools produces concordances in a few clicks. If you know how to program one, you can adapt the pattern and the corpus to your own question: compare how Kateb Yacine and Mohammed Dib use a word, follow a term through a novel and its translation, or list the recurring formulas of a political speech.

Handling simple reports: Excel and PDF

You must be able to share an analysis with a thesis supervisor or an examination panel. Two formats dominate: the Excel workbook, which anyone can open, sort and add to, and the PDF, which is fixed and ready to print. The examples reuse the DataFrame df from lesson 3.

Exporting to Excel

to_excel() writes an .xlsx file, provided that the openpyxl library is installed; index=False leaves out the row numbers. For several sheets in the same workbook, you open a pd.ExcelWriter in a with block: each to_excel() creates a sheet named by sheet_name. read_excel() reads a sheet back.

agg(["count", "mean"]) calculates several statistics per group. The genre index, written to the "Par genre" sheet, becomes an ordinary column again when the file is read back.

Creating a PDF with fpdf2

The fpdf2 library builds a document page by page. Install it with pip install fpdf2, not fpdf, which is an old, abandoned version; the import is written from fpdf import FPDF. cell() writes one line inside a rectangle (width 0 means up to the right margin; the height comes next), and multi_cell() writes a paragraph that moves to a new line automatically. new_x="LMARGIN", new_y="NEXT" brings the cursor back to the start of the next line.

itertuples() goes through the DataFrame row by row; each row gives access to its columns as attributes (ligne.titre). The resulting PDF contains a bold title, the list of the ten works and the final note.

Helvetica knows neither Arabic nor "œ"

The fonts built into fpdf2 (Helvetica, Times, Courier) are limited to the Latin-1 character set: French accents are included, but not the ligature "œ", the typographic apostrophe "’" or the Arabic alphabet. pdf.cell(0, 10, "Les œuvres") raises an FPDFUnicodeEncodingException error. You then need to load a Unicode font in TTF format with pdf.add_font().

For Arabic, choose a font that contains this alphabet, such as Amiri or Noto Naskh Arabic (free on Google Fonts), and place the .ttf file next to the script. Also turn on text shaping (mise en forme du texte), which joins the letters according to their position in the word and writes from right to left; it requires the uharfbuzz package.

Reading a PDF with pypdf

To extract the text from a PDF, the pypdf library provides PdfReader: its pages attribute is a list of pages, and extract_text() returns the text of one page.

Extraction assumes that the PDF has a text layer. A scanned book is only a series of images: in that case, you need optical character recognition (OCR, reconnaissance optique de caractères). Always check the result: columns and footnotes may come out in the wrong order.

Symbolic AI: expert systems

Symbolic AI represents knowledge with symbols that a human can read, facts and rules, rather than with numbers learned from data. Its best-known product is the expert system (système expert), a program that reproduces the reasoning of a specialist in a narrow field. The approach is familiar to literature students: a treatise on versification that states "a sonnet has fourteen lines, divided into two quatrains and two tercets" is already stating a rule.

The three components

Fact base
What we know about the situation under study ("the poem has 14 lines"). It grows as the reasoning goes on.
Rule base
The knowledge of the field, in the form IF premises THEN conclusion; the premises are joined by AND, also written ∩.
Inference engine
The program that finds the applicable rules, fires them and adds their conclusions to the fact base. It does not depend on the field: you can move from medicine to poetry by changing only the rules.

Forward and backward chaining

Forward chaining

Forward chaining (chaînage avant) is data-driven. You start from the facts, fire a rule whose premises are all known, add its conclusion, and start again until you reach the goal or until no rule produces a new fact.

  • From the facts to the goal: "what can I deduce?"
  • Drawback: it can deduce many useless facts.

Backward chaining

Backward chaining (chaînage arrière) is goal-driven. You start from the hypothesis to be proved and look for the rules that conclude it. Their premises become sub-goals, until you get back to known facts. It is well suited to diagnosis.

  • From the goal to the facts: "what do I need to prove this?"
  • Advantage: only the rules useful for the goal are examined.

Conflict resolution and non-deducible facts

When several rules can be applied at the same time, the engine must choose one: this is conflict resolution. The strategy used in the tutorial (TD) follows a rule order fixed in advance: in each cycle, you fire the first applicable rule in this order, then start again from the top of the list. A rule that has already fired does not fire a second time. Non-deducible facts do not appear in the conclusion of any rule: only observation or the user can provide them (H, G and K in the tutorial exercise). In backward chaining, a non-deducible sub-goal that is missing from the base makes that path fail, or leads the system to ask the user.

Example: recognizing a poetic form

The Italian or French sonnet (Petrarch, Ronsard, Baudelaire) has two quatrains and two tercets; the English sonnet, also called the Shakespearean sonnet, has three quatrains and a couplet. Let us translate these definitions into rules. The observations ("14 lines", "2 tercets"…) are the non-deducible facts.

Let us run forward chaining on Joachim Du Bellay's sonnet "Heureux qui, comme Ulysse, a fait un beau voyage" (Les Regrets, 1558).

  1. Initial fact base

    {14 lines, 2 quatrains, 2 tercets, alexandrines}. No rule uses "alexandrines": this fact will stay inactive.

  2. Cycle 1

    R1 fails (no "3 lines"); R2 applies: we add "sonnet".

  3. Cycle 2

    We start again from R1. R1 and R3 fail, R2 has already fired; R4 applies: we add "Italian or French sonnet".

  4. Cycle 3

    R5 fails (no "1 couplet"); R6 applies: we add "fixed form".

  5. Cycle 4: stop

    No rule produces a new fact any more. Three facts have been deduced.

In backward chaining, the hypothesis "English sonnet?" would require, by R5, "sonnet" and "1 couplet". This last fact is non-deducible and missing from the base: the hypothesis is rejected at once.

In Python, each rule is a pair (premises, conclusion). The expression premisses <= faits tests whether all the premises are in the base. Requiring a new conclusion prevents a rule from firing twice. The else of the for loop runs when no rule has fired.

Three historical expert systems

DENDRAL

Stanford, from 1965 (Edward Feigenbaum, Joshua Lederberg, Bruce Buchanan): inferring the structure of organic molecules from mass spectra. It is often regarded as the first expert system.

MYCIN

Stanford, 1970s (Edward Shortliffe): diagnosing bacterial blood infections and proposing an antibiotic. Backward chaining, "certainty factors", explanation of its own questions. It was never used routinely in hospitals.

XCON (R1)

John McDermott (Carnegie Mellon) for Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC), early 1980s: configuring orders for VAX computers. Forward chaining, several thousand rules.

Strengths and limits

The strength of symbolic AI is explainability: each conclusion is justified by the exact chain of rules that fired. This makes the reasoning traceable and easy to correct, without large amounts of data. Yet two limits caused the decline of expert systems at the end of the 1980s. The knowledge acquisition bottleneck: experts know how to do more than they can put into words, and turning their knowledge into rules takes months of interviews. Brittleness: outside the cases it was designed for, the system fails. A sonnet printed as a single block, or with its stanzas in reverse order as some 19th-century poets wrote them, fires no rule, whereas a reader recognizes the form at once.

Machine learning (apprentissage automatique)

Much knowledge cannot easily be turned into rules: you recognize Kateb Yacine's prose within a few lines, but you cannot list the rules behind that judgment. Machine learning (apprentissage automatique, ML) reverses the approach: you give the program examples, and it finds regularities in them by itself. According to Tom Mitchell's definition (1997), paraphrased here, a program learns when its performance on a task, measured in a given way, improves with experience. In practice, learning means adjusting the parameters of a model, that is, numbers, until it reproduces the known examples as well as possible.

Features
The characteristics of each example, expressed as numbers (attributs): number of words, presence of the word "free".
Label
The known correct answer for an example (étiquette): "spam" or "non-spam". Labels exist only in supervised learning.
Model
The formula that turns the features into a prediction.
Training
Adjusting the parameters on examples (entraînement).
Prediction
Applying the trained model to a new example.

Measuring the quality of the predictions on examples the model has never seen.

Evaluating a model

A model is never evaluated on the examples used to train it, just as a student is not judged on questions whose answers they know by heart. The corpus is split into a training set (often 70 to 80% of the examples) and a test set, with scikit-learn's train_test_split function. Suppose a spam filter is tested on 100 emails, 40 of which are spam. The confusion matrix crosses the true category with the prediction; "spam", the category we are looking for, is called positive.

Confusion matrix of a spam filter on 100 emails (made-up figures)
True categoryPredicted "spam"Predicted "non-spam"
Spam (40)30 true positives (TP)10 false negatives (FN)
Non-spam (60)2 false positives (FP)58 true negatives (TN)

accuracy = (TP + TN) / total = (30 + 58) / 100 = 0.88

precision = TP / (TP + FP) = 30 / 32 ≈ 0.94

recall = TP / (TP + FN) = 30 / 40 = 0.75

Accuracy (exactitude) is the share of emails that are correctly classified. Precision tells you whether the filter is right when it flags a spam: it is right 94% of the time, so it almost never sends your thesis supervisor's message to the trash. Recall tells you what share of the spams it catches: 75%, so one spam in four still gets through. The two measures often pull in opposite directions. Be careful with imbalanced classes: if 5% of emails are spam, labeling everything "non-spam" gives 95% accuracy with a recall of zero.

Overfitting

Overfitting (surapprentissage) happens when the model also learns the accidents of its examples: if all the training spams happened to contain the word "Thursday", the announcement of your Thursday seminar would end up in the trash. Symptom: excellent results on the training set, much worse ones on the test set. Remedies: more data, a simpler model, fewer features.

Email classification: "spam" or "non-spam"

First, the texts must be turned into numbers. The bag of words (sac de mots) describes each message by the number of times each word of the vocabulary occurs in it, without taking word order into account: "click here, click fast" becomes {click: 2, here: 1, fast: 1}. The naive Bayes classifier learns how often each word appears in spams and in the other messages. It then combines the clues from all the words of a new message to choose the most likely category. It is "naive" because it treats words as independent, which is false but works well.

P(spam | message) ∝ P(spam) × P(word₁ | spam) × … × P(wordₙ | spam)

The symbol ∝ means "proportional to"; the same calculation is done for "non-spam", and the two results are compared.

The verb "gagner" does not appear in the training corpus, which only contains "gagnez" and "gagné": the deciding words are "cliquez", "ici", "gratuit" and "un". Lemmatization with spaCy would group these forms together.

Recommendations

Content-based filtering recommends books whose characteristics (author, genre, keywords) are similar to those of the books the reader has already borrowed: after Nedjma, Le Polygone étoilé by the same author, Kateb Yacine. Collaborative filtering (filtrage collaboratif) ignores the content and relies on other readers: "people who borrowed the same books as you also borrowed…". To compare two readers, we represent each of them as a list of 0s and 1s, one value per book, and compute their cosine similarity: 1 for identical borrowings, 0 if they have no book in common.

cos(A, B) = (A · B) / (‖A‖ × ‖B‖)

Image identification (e.g. faces)

For a computer, an image is a table of numbers: 28 × 28 pixels in grayscale make 784 values between 0 (black) and 255 (white); in color, each pixel has three values (red, green, blue). For a long time, the features (edges, contrasts, distance between the eyes) were designed by hand; this is how the method of Paul Viola and Michael Jones (2001) detected faces in real time. Since 2012, deep learning has dominated, because networks learn their own features from millions of images. We distinguish detection (where is the face?), verification (is it the same person?) and identification (who is it?).

Facial recognition: an ethical issue

Large-scale face identification makes permanent surveillance of public spaces possible, without consent. This threatens privacy and can discourage people from demonstrating. It also raises a problem of bias. In 2018, the "Gender Shades" study by Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru tested three commercial gender classification systems on photographs of African and European members of parliament: error rates reached 34.7% for darker-skinned women, compared with at most 0.8% for lighter-skinned men, mainly because of imbalanced training data. The European Union's regulation on AI (AI Act), adopted in 2024, in principle bans "real-time" remote biometric identification in public spaces for law enforcement purposes, with strictly regulated exceptions.

Some machine learning algorithms

The data in the examples are made up and tiny, so that you can redo the calculations by hand.

a. Linear regression

Linear regression predicts a number by drawing the straight line that passes as close as possible to a cloud of points: for example, the time needed to read a novel, based on its number of pages.

ŷ = w · x + b

x is the number of pages, ŷ ("y hat") the predicted time, w the slope (in hours per page) and b a fixed time. The least squares method chooses the values of w and b that minimize the mean of the squared differences between the real times yᵢ and the predicted times ŷᵢ. Squaring stops the differences from canceling each other out and penalizes large errors.

J(w, b) = (1/n) × Σ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)²

One page takes about 0.020 hours, a little more than a minute: for 250 pages, 0.0203 × 250 + 0.45 ≈ 5.5 hours. The line is only valid within the observed range. With several variables, ŷ = w₁ · x₁ + w₂ · x₂ + … + b, this is called multiple regression (mini-project 4).

b. Gradient descent

Most models cannot be solved in one go: their parameters are found step by step with gradient descent (descente de gradient). Imagine a hiker in the fog: they cannot see the valley, but they can feel the slope under their feet, and each time they take a step downhill. The valley is the cost function J(w), which measures the error for each value of the parameter w; the slope is its gradient.

w ← w − η · ∂J/∂w

The arrow means "replace with". If the slope ∂J/∂w is positive, w decreases; if it is negative, w increases. The number η (eta) is the learning rate (taux d'apprentissage), the length of the step. Take J(w) = (w − 3)², which is smallest at w = 3 and has the slope 2 · (w − 3). Starting from w = 0 with η = 0.1, the slope is −6, so w becomes 0 − 0.1 × (−6) = 0.6, then 1.08, and so on towards 3.

With a step that is too large (η = 1.1), w jumps over the minimum (6.6, then −1.32, then 8.18) and moves further and further away: the algorithm diverges. With a step that is too small (η = 0.01), w only reaches 0.29 after five steps. A real model has thousands or billions of parameters, but the principle is the same.

c. Classification based on features

Stylometry measures style with statistics, especially for authorship attribution. Function words (articles, prepositions, conjunctions) are very useful here, because each writer uses them in a regular and mostly unconscious way, whatever the subject. The field is very real: in 1964, Frederick Mosteller and David Wallace attributed the twelve disputed Federalist Papers to James Madison; in 2019, Florian Cafiero and Jean-Baptiste Camps concluded that Molière really was the author of his plays. Let us describe each excerpt by its average sentence length and its share of function words.

The k-nearest neighbors method (k-NN, k plus proches voisins) looks for the k excerpts by known authors that are closest to the anonymous excerpt, and lets them vote. Closeness is measured by the ordinary distance:

d(x, y) = √((x₁ − y₁)² + (x₂ − y₂)²)

The anonymous excerpt (27, 46) is at a distance of √8 ≈ 2.8 from (29, 44), 3 from (30, 46) and √29 ≈ 5.4 from (32, 48), three excerpts by author A: the vote points to A. If the features are not on the same scale, they are standardized first. The decision tree works differently: it learns a series of questions of the form "feature ≤ threshold?". It is an IF … THEN rule base, but one learned from data.

The tree needs only one question: B's sentences are short and A's are long. The share of function words overlaps between the two authors, so the tree does not use it.

d. Clustering (k-means)

Clustering (regroupement) forms groups of similar objects without any labels: it is unsupervised learning, and the tool for mini-project 2 on surveys. The k-means algorithm (French: k-moyennes) works as follows:

  1. Choose k

    Set the number of groups you want, for example k = 3.

  2. Place k centers

    Choose k starting points, the centroids, often at random among the data.

  3. Assign

    Each individual joins the group of the nearest centroid.

  4. Recompute the centers

    Each centroid is moved to the mean of the individuals in its group.

  5. Repeat

    Repeat steps 3 and 4 until no individual changes group any more.

To choose k, we compute the inertia: the sum of the squared distances between each individual and the center of its group. It always decreases as k increases. The elbow method keeps the value of k after which it hardly decreases any more.

inertia = Σ distance(individual, center of its group)²

The elbow is at k = 3. The group numbers are arbitrary: it is up to the researcher to interpret each center (heavy readers, heavy screen users, intermediate profiles) and to name the groups.

e. Other algorithms

SVM

Support vector machines (SVC) look for the boundary that separates two categories with the widest possible margin. For a long time they were among the best text classifiers.

Random forests

A random forest (RandomForestClassifier) makes hundreds of decision trees vote. Each tree is trained on a random selection of the examples and features.

Dimensionality reduction (PCA)

Principal component analysis (PCA) summarizes dozens of variables in two or three axes that keep most of their spread, so that a survey or a corpus can be shown on a flat plane.

Deep learning (apprentissage profond)

Deep learning (apprentissage profond) is the branch of machine learning that uses artificial neural networks with many layers. It is behind the recent progress in image and speech recognition, translation and text generation. The comparison with the brain is only a loose one: an artificial neuron is just a small calculation.

The artificial neuron

A neuron multiplies each of its inputs by a weight, adds everything up, adds a bias, then applies an activation function.

z = w₁ · x₁ + w₂ · x₂ + w₃ · x₃ + b

output = f(z)

The most common activation function, ReLU, is f(z) = max(0, z). With x = (2, 1, 0), w = (0.5, −1, 2) and b = 0.5: z = 1 − 1 + 0 + 0.5 = 0.5, and the output is 0.5. Frank Rosenblatt's perceptron (1958) was a neuron of this kind.

Layers and depth

Neurons form layers: an input layer, hidden layers and an output layer. A network is deep when it stacks many hidden layers. Each layer builds a more abstract representation than the one before: for an image, edges, then shapes, then parts of objects. Nobody programs these stages; they emerge from training.

Learning: backpropagation, data and GPUs

The weights start with random values. For each example, the network computes its answer, and the error is measured against the expected answer. Then we go back from the output to the input to give each weight its share of responsibility. This is backpropagation (rétropropagation), made popular in 1986 by David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton and Ronald Williams. Each weight is then corrected by gradient descent, and the process is repeated on millions of examples.

These old ideas only became effective in the early 2010s, thanks to massive data (millions of annotated images, the text of the Web) and to GPUs, graphics processors that carry out huge numbers of multiplications in parallel. In 2012, the AlexNet network by Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton won the ImageNet image recognition competition by a clear margin. The smallest version of GPT-2 (2019) already has more than a hundred million parameters, and training large models uses a lot of energy.

Two classic architectures: CNNs and RNNs

Convolutional networks (CNN)

They were designed for images, building on Yann LeCun's work on handwritten digits (late 1980s). They slide small filters across the image: grids of weights, 3 × 3 for example. At each position, the filter computes a weighted sum of the pixels: the response is strong where the pattern it encodes is present.

  • What a filter "sees": first edges and contrasts, then, deeper in the network, textures, letters, eyes.
  • Uses in the humanities and social sciences (SHS): transcribing manuscripts, analyzing photographs.

Recurrent networks (RNN, LSTM)

They read a sequence one element at a time, one word after another, and update a hidden state, a kind of memory of what came before. LSTMs (Sepp Hochreiter and Jürgen Schmidhuber, 1997) add "gates" that decide what to keep and what to forget.

  • Uses: translation, speech, text generation before 2017.
  • Limits: reading is sequential, and therefore slow; memory fades over long texts.

Text generation with Transformers

In 2017, the paper "Attention Is All You Need" (Ashish Vaswani and his co-authors, Google) introduced the Transformer. It processes all the words of a passage at the same time and computes the relations between them with an attention mechanism. Three notions help to understand it.

Tokens
The text is split into tokens (French: "jetons"): words, parts of words, punctuation marks. A rare word is cut into several pieces; the GPT-2 vocabulary has a little over 50,000 tokens.
Embeddings
Each token becomes a vector of several hundred numbers, its embedding (plongement). Words used in similar contexts get vectors that are close to each other. This is the distributional hypothesis, which the linguist J. R. Firth summed up in 1957 as "You shall know a word by the company it keeps".
Attention
Each token computes how much it should "look at" each of the other tokens. In "The student handed her thesis to the professor because she had finished", the token "she" should look mainly at "student".

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(Q · Kᵀ / √d) · V

Each token sends a query (Q), shows a key (K) and carries a value (V). The product Q · Kᵀ measures how well queries and keys match; it is divided by √d to keep the values moderate, and softmax turns the result into proportions that add up to 1.

Encoders, such as BERT (Google, 2018), read the sentence in both directions and are used to understand a text: classification, sentiment, named entities in a political speech (mini-project 3). There are encoders for French (CamemBERT) and for Arabic (AraBERT). Decoders, such as the GPT series (OpenAI, since 2018), read from left to right and predict the next token: they are the models that generate text.

Generating text is a loop: the model computes a probability for each token in the vocabulary, one token is drawn at random according to these probabilities and added to the text, and the process starts again. The temperature T controls this draw, using the model's raw scores zᵢ:

p(tokenᵢ) = exp(zᵢ / T) / Σⱼ exp(zⱼ / T)

With T < 1, the differences grow and the text becomes predictable; with T > 1, they flatten out and the text becomes surprising, then incoherent. To run the examples: pip install transformers torch; the model is downloaded the first time you run the code.

Hallucinations and sources

A generative model produces the most plausible continuation, not the most truthful one. It can confidently invent a quotation, a date or a complete bibliographic reference that does not exist: this is called a hallucination. Reinforcement learning (apprentissage par renforcement)

Reinforcement learning (apprentissage par renforcement, RL) does not need labeled examples: an agent acts, observes the consequences and receives rewards or penalties. It learns by trial and error, the way we learn to ride a bike.

Agent
The one that makes decisions: a game program, a robot, a conversational agent.
Environment
The world it interacts with: a board, a room, a user.
State (s)
The situation at a given moment.
Action (a)
What the agent can do in this state.
Reward (r)
The number sent back after the action, positive or negative.
Policy (π)
The strategy that says which action to choose in each state: this is what is learned.

Explore or exploit?

Exploitation means choosing the action that has worked best so far; exploration means trying a less familiar one. Rereading an author you love is a safe choice; but if you never open other books, you will never discover your next favorite authors. The strategy called ε-greedy chooses the best known action, except in a small proportion ε of cases (10%, for example), where it picks an action at random.

Q-learning

In Q-learning (Christopher Watkins, 1989), the agent keeps a table Q(s, a). For each state and each action, this table estimates the total reward the agent can expect. After each action, the agent corrects the corresponding cell:

Q(s, a) ← Q(s, a) + α · [r + γ · max Q(s′, a′) − Q(s, a)]

Q(s, a)
The current estimate of the value of action a in state s.
α (alpha)
The learning rate, between 0 and 1: the share of the new experience that is taken into account.
r
The immediate reward.
γ (gamma)
The discount factor, between 0 and 1: close to 0, the agent only thinks about the immediate gain; close to 1, it aims for the long term.
max Q(s′, a′)
The best value that can be expected from the new state s′, among all the possible actions a′.
Brackets
The target r + γ · max Q(s′, a′), minus the old estimate: this is the "surprise", and a fraction α of it is corrected.

An agent is exploring a maze. At junction s, it turns right, receives r = 1 and arrives in s′, where the best known action is worth 4. Before this step, Q(s, right) = 2; we take α = 0.5 and γ = 0.9.

  1. Target

    r + γ · max Q(s′, a′) = 1 + 0.9 × 4 = 4.6.

  2. Surprise

    4.6 − 2 = 2.6.

  3. Correction

    α × 2.6 = 0.5 × 2.6 = 1.3.

  4. New value

    Q(s, right) = 2 + 1.3 = 3.3.

The action turned out to be better than expected: its value goes up, but only halfway to the target, so that the agent does not overreact to a single experience.

Applications

  • Games. In March 2016, AlphaGo (DeepMind) beat the South Korean go player Lee Sedol by four games to one. It combined deep networks, tree search and reinforcement learning based on games played against itself.
  • Robotics. A robot learns to walk or to grasp objects by trial and error, usually first in simulation, because real trials are slow and expensive.
  • Conversational agents. RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback) was used to fine-tune InstructGPT (OpenAI, 2022) and then ChatGPT. Human annotators compare several answers, a "reward model" learns their preferences, and the language model is fine-tuned by reinforcement learning to maximize this reward. A question for the humanities and social sciences (SHS): which values, which culture and which language do these preferences pass on?

Key points

  • An expert system combines a fact base, a rule base and an inference engine; forward chaining goes from the facts to the goal, backward chaining from the goal to the facts.
  • An ML model is trained on a training set and evaluated on a test set (accuracy, precision, recall); watch out for overfitting.
  • Regression predicts a number, classification predicts a category, and clustering forms groups without labels; gradient descent adjusts the parameters.
  • Deep learning stacks layers trained by backpropagation: CNNs for images, RNNs for sequences, Transformers for language. Generating text means predicting the next token: always check the output.
  • Reinforcement learning learns a policy from rewards; the families can be combined, as in AlphaGo or RLHF.

Exercise 1 · Review the course concepts

Review the concepts from the chapter 1 lecture, then go deeper into supervised learning and unsupervised learning. For each one, give a definition, the type of data required, typical tasks and one practical difficulty.

Show the solution

Here is one possible summary. Complete it with your lecture notes.

Artificial intelligence
A field of computer science that designs programs able to perform tasks we associate with human intelligence: reasoning, understanding a language, recognizing an image, deciding. Weak AI (or narrow AI) specializes in one task or one domain. Current systems belong to this category, even if the most versatile ones revive the debate. Strong AI (or general AI) refers to an AI able to perform any human intellectual task. It remains a goal and a subject of debate.
Two main approaches
The symbolic approach: humans write down knowledge in the form of rules (this is the case of expert systems). Machine learning (apprentissage automatique): the program derives its own rules from a large number of examples.
Supervised learning
Data: examples paired with the expected answer, called a label (an email and the tag "spam" or "not spam"). Principle: the model learns the link between the input and the label, then applies it to new cases. Tasks: classification (predicting a category) and regression (predicting a numerical value). Evaluation: the predictions are compared with the true labels of examples set aside, the test set. Difficulty: annotating takes time, and the labels reflect the choices, and sometimes the prejudices, of the annotators.
Unsupervised learning
Data: examples without labels. Principle: the model looks for a structure in the data on its own. Tasks: clustering (grouping similar cases), dimensionality reduction (summarizing many variables in a few axes), anomaly detection, topic modeling. Evaluation: there is no "right answer"; we judge how coherent the groups are and how useful they are for interpretation. Difficulty: naming and interpreting the groups obtained.
In between
Semi-supervised learning combines a few labeled examples with many raw examples. Self-supervised learning takes the label from the data itself, for example by hiding the next word of a sentence and asking the model to guess it: this is how large language models are pre-trained.
Reinforcement learning
There are no corrected examples: an agent acts in an environment and receives rewards or penalties. Over time, it learns the strategy that maximizes its rewards.

The right question to ask

For each example, do we have the expected answer? If so, the learning is supervised. If not, it is unsupervised. If the program learns by acting and receiving rewards, it is reinforcement learning.

Questions: a) Draw the graph that corresponds to the rules. b) Work through the system step by step in forward chaining.

Show the solution

a) The rule graph. Each fact is shown as a rectangle and each rule as a circle. An arrow goes from each condition to the rule, then from the rule to each of its conclusions. The arrows that enter the same rule are joined by AND: the rule fires only if all its conditions are established. When two rules lead to the same fact (R3 and R7 for A, R4 and R6 for C), it is enough for one of them to fire: these paths are joined by OR. This is why we call it an AND/OR graph.

The graph gives a first diagnosis: no arrow points to G, H or K, which are the non-deducible facts. Since G is not in the initial base either, rules R7 and R4, which need it, can never fire. The only possible path to C therefore goes through R6.

b) Working through forward chaining. The rules are examined in the required order (R3, R7, R1, R4, R5, R2, R6). A rule fires if all its conditions are in the fact base; its conclusions are then added to the base. A rule that has already fired is not run again. We stop as soon as the goal is in the base, or when a complete pass over the rules adds nothing new.

Exercise 2 a · Linear regression

Linear regression looks for the line ŷ = a·x + b that comes as close as possible to a cloud of points. The least squares method chooses a and b so that the sum of the squared differences between the observed values y and the predicted values ŷ is as small as possible. It gives:

Exercise 2 b · Gradient descent

Gradient descent looks for the minimum of an error function J by moving step by step in the direction in which J decreases. At each step:

wₖ₊₁ = wₖ − η · J′(wₖ)

where J′ is the derivative of J and η (eta) is the learning rate (taux d'apprentissage), which sets the length of the step. Let J(w) = (w − 3)², w₀ = 0 and η = 0.1. Calculate w₁ and w₂, as well as J(w₀), J(w₁) and J(w₂). Toward which value does the sequence move?

Show the solution

The derivative is J′(w) = 2(w − 3).

Step 1: J′(0) = 2 × (0 − 3) = −6 w₁ = 0 − 0.1 × (−6) = 0.6

Step 2: J′(0.6) = 2 × (0.6 − 3) = −4.8 w₂ = 0.6 − 0.1 × (−4.8) = 0.6 + 0.48 = 1.08

Error values: J(w₀) = (0 − 3)² = 9; J(w₁) = (−2.4)² = 5.76; J(w₂) = (−1.92)² = 3.6864. The error decreases at each step.

The sequence moves toward w = 3, where J is at its minimum (J(3) = 0). You can see this by rewriting the rule: wₖ₊₁ − 3 = (1 − 2η)(wₖ − 3) = 0.8 (wₖ − 3). At each step, the distance to the minimum is multiplied by 0.8: 3, 2.4, 1.92, then 1.536, which gives w₃ = 1.464, and so on.

The role of the learning rate: with η = 1, the factor becomes 1 − 2 = −1, and w oscillates endlessly between 0 and 6. With η > 1, the oscillations grow and the method diverges. On the other hand, a step that is too small makes learning very slow.

Exercise 4 b · Deep learning: CNNs and RNNs

A convolutional network (CNN) slides small filters over an image. The stride (pas) is how far the filter shifts at each move. The padding (marge) is the number of rows of zeros added around the image. A recurrent network (RNN) reads a sequence, for example a text, element by element, while keeping a memory of what it has read.

  1. A 28 × 28 pixel grayscale image goes through a convolution layer with a 3 × 3 filter, a stride of 1 and no padding. What is the size of the output? Same question with a padding of 1. Bonus: with a stride of 2 and no padding.
  2. The filter F = [[1, 0, −1], [1, 0, −1], [1, 0, −1]] is applied to the image area P = [[1, 0, 2], [0, 1, 0], [3, 0, 1]]. Calculate the value obtained (the sum of the element-wise products).
  3. Why does a simple RNN struggle with long texts? What did LSTMs, and then the attention mechanism, bring?
Show the solution

1. Size of the output. For an image of side n, a filter of side f, a padding p and a stride s:

side of the output = ⌊(n + 2p − f) / s⌋ + 1

  • No padding, stride 1: (28 + 0 − 3) / 1 + 1 = 25 + 1 = 26, which gives a 26 × 26 output.
  • Padding of 1, stride 1: (28 + 2 − 3) / 1 + 1 = 27 + 1 = 28, which gives 28 × 28: the image keeps its size.
  • Bonus, no padding, stride 2: ⌊25 / 2⌋ + 1 = 12 + 1 = 13, which gives 13 × 13.

A 3 × 3 filter on a grayscale image has 9 weights and 1 bias, so 10 parameters to learn, whatever the size of the image.

2. A convolution. Multiply element by element, row by row, then add up:

row 1: 1×1 + 0×0 + 2×(−1) = −1 row 2: 0×1 + 1×0 + 0×(−1) = 0 row 3: 3×1 + 0×0 + 1×(−1) = 2

total: −1 + 0 + 2 = 1

This filter subtracts the right-hand column (2 + 0 + 1 = 3) from the left-hand column (1 + 0 + 3 = 4). It responds to vertical contrasts, that is, to vertical edges in the image. A CNN learns the values of its filters by itself.

3. RNNs and long texts. An RNN summarizes everything it has read in a fixed-size memory, updated at each word. During training, the error correction travels back through the sequence word by word. At each step, it is multiplied by factors that are often smaller than 1, so it becomes tiny for the first words. This is the vanishing gradient (disparition du gradient) problem. As a result, the network has trouble linking a pronoun to a character named three paragraphs earlier. In addition, reading word by word prevents the calculations from being run in parallel.

  • LSTMs (Long Short-Term Memory, Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997) add a memory protected by "gates" that decide what to keep, what to forget and what to pass on. Information travels over much longer distances.
  • The attention mechanism (Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio, 2014), and then the Transformer architecture (Vaswani and colleagues, 2017), allow each word to look directly at all the other words in the sequence, whatever their distance, and allow all the words to be processed in parallel. This is the basis of BERT and of the GPT models. The trade-off: the cost of computation grows with the square of the text length.

Exercise 4 c · Reinforcement learning: one Q-learning update

In Q-learning, an agent estimates, for each situation s and each action a, a value Q(s, a): the total reward it can expect if it does a in situation s. After doing action a, receiving reward r and arriving in situation s′, it corrects its estimate:

Q(s, a) ← Q(s, a) + α · [ r + γ · maxₐ′ Q(s′, a′) − Q(s, a) ]

α is the learning rate and γ (gamma) is the discount factor, which sets the weight of future rewards. An agent moves through a maze. We have Q(s, a) = 2, α = 0.5, γ = 0.9, r = 1; in the new situation s′, Q(s′, a₁) = 4 and Q(s′, a₂) = 3. 1. Calculate the new value of Q(s, a). 2. Same question if the action had cost a penalty, r = −1.

Show the solution

1. With r = 1.

  • Best value in s′: max(4, 3) = 4.
  • Target: r + γ × 4 = 1 + 0.9 × 4 = 1 + 3.6 = 4.6.
  • Difference between the target and the current estimate: 4.6 − 2 = 2.6.
  • New value: Q(s, a) = 2 + 0.5 × 2.6 = 2 + 1.3 = 3.3.

2. With r = −1. Target: −1 + 3.6 = 2.6; difference: 2.6 − 2 = 0.6; new value: 2 + 0.5 × 0.6 = 2.3.

Even with a penalty, the value of the action increases slightly, because the action leads to a promising situation (value 4): γ makes the future count in the present judgment. With α = 0.5, the agent only covers half of the gap after each experience, which avoids calling everything into question after a single try. In practice, the agent must also explore: from time to time, it chooses a random action rather than the best known one, in order to discover other paths.

Preparing and running a round table

A good round table is not a series of presentations. It is an exchange in which everyone listens, responds and adds nuance. The following procedure will help you get there.

  1. Form the group and share out the roles

    One to two weeks before the session, form a group of five or six people and choose the moderator, the speakers and the rapporteur. Everyone knows what is expected of them.

  2. Define the question

    Choose two of the four questions suggested and turn them into one clear research question, stated in a single sentence.

  3. Do the reading

    Each speaker reads at least one of the references listed and notes two arguments and one concrete example, with their exact source. Any information obtained from an AI tool is checked against an independent source.

  4. Prepare your contribution

    Prepare an opening statement of three minutes at most, on a card with key ideas, not a text to read aloud. Anticipate one objection and the answer you would give.

  5. Run the debate

    The moderator introduces the question and the participants, gives each person the floor, keeps the discussion going, watches the time and opens the discussion to the class for the last fifteen minutes.

  6. Report back

    The rapporteur writes a one-page summary: points of agreement, disagreements, questions that remain open, and references cited. It is shared with the whole class.

Checklist before and after the session

  • The question under debate fits in one clear sentence.
  • Everyone knows the roles before the session.
  • Each speaker relies on at least one identified source.
  • The examples come from real corpora or real situations.
  • Opposing positions have been presented fairly.
  • Speaking time has been shared evenly among the participants.
  • Any use of an AI tool during preparation is declared.
  • The summary separates agreements, disagreements and open questions.

Glossary of the artificial intelligence terms used in the course
Term (EN)FrenchShort definition
AgentagentA system that perceives its environment and acts on it to achieve a goal.
AlgorithmalgorithmeA finite sequence of precise instructions for solving a problem or carrying out a task.
Artificial general intelligence (AGI), strong AIIA générale (forte)A hypothetical system able to perform any human intellectual task; in Searle's sense, one that has a real mind.
Attention mechanismattention (mécanisme d')A technique that lets a network weigh the importance of each word in a sequence in relation to the others; it is at the heart of Transformers.
Backward chainingchaînage arrièreReasoning that starts from the goal to be proved and works back to the facts needed to establish it.
BiasbiaisA systematic distortion in the results of a model, often inherited from unbalanced data or from design choices.
CentralitycentralitéA measure of how important a node is in a network, for example based on its number of links (degree) or on its position as an intermediary.
ClassificationclassificationA supervised task that consists of assigning a category to an item (spam or not spam, the period of a poem).
ClusteringpartitionnementUnsupervised grouping of similar items into groups called clusters.
Convolutional neural networkréseau convolutif (CNN)A neural network that applies local filters; widely used for images, and sometimes for texts.
CorpuscorpusA set of texts brought together according to explicit criteria in order to be studied.
Deep learningapprentissage profondMachine learning based on neural networks with many layers.
Expert systemsystème expertA symbolic AI program that reasons using a fact base, a rule base and an inference engine.
Fact basebase de faitsIn an expert system, the set of facts that are known or have been deduced at a given point in the reasoning.
Forward chainingchaînage avantReasoning that starts from known facts and applies the rules to deduce new facts, until the goal is reached.
Gradient descentdescente de gradientAn optimization method that adjusts the parameters of a model step by step, in the direction that reduces the error the most.
HallucinationhallucinationA false or invented statement that a generative model presents in a plausible way.
Inference enginemoteur d'inférenceThe program in an expert system that applies the rules to the facts to produce new conclusions.
LabelétiquetteThe correct answer associated with an example in a supervised dataset.
Language modelmodèle de langageA model that estimates the probability of a sequence of words and can predict or generate how a text continues.
LemmatizationlemmatisationReducing a word to its dictionary form ("chantaient" becomes "chanter").
Machine learning (ML)apprentissage automatiqueA set of methods by which a program improves its performance from examples, rather than from rules written by hand.
Named entityentité nomméeAn expression that refers to a specific entity: a person, place, organization or date.
Narrow AI (weak AI)IA étroite (faible)An AI system designed for one specific task, which cannot transfer its skill to other fields.
Natural language processing (NLP)traitement automatique des langues (TAL, TALN)A field of AI and linguistics that designs methods to analyze, understand and produce texts in human language.
Neural networkréseau de neuronesA model made of computing units (artificial neurons) organized in layers and connected by weights that are adjusted during learning.
Overfittingsur-apprentissageA flaw of a model that memorizes the details of the training set and generalizes poorly to new data.
PerceptronperceptronA model of an artificial neuron proposed by Frank Rosenblatt in 1958, able to learn a linear separation between two classes.
