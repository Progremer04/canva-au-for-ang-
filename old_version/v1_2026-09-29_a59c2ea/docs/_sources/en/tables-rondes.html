Round tables

A round table brings a small group of students together around an open question, in front of the class. You will learn to build a well-documented argument, to defend it orally and to do justice to positions you do not share.

Each round table lasts about 45 minutes. Four to six participants, guided by a moderator, debate for about thirty minutes, and then the discussion opens to the rest of the class. Afterwards, a rapporteur writes a summary that is shared with everyone. The four themes below are taken from the syllabus. Each one comes with starting questions, a division of roles and reading suggestions.

The origins and emergence of AI

The dream of thinking machines is much older than the computer. It runs through the automata of al-Jazari (1206), Mary Shelley's Frankenstein (1818) and Karel Čapek's play R.U.R. (1920), which gave the world the word "robot". Yet scientific AI was born in a specific context, between Turing's article (1950) and the Dartmouth workshop (1956). This round table explores the link between the literary imagination and the scientific project.

Questions for the debate

  • Why does Turing replace the question "Can machines think?" with an imitation game? What do we gain, and what do we lose, in this shift?
  • Do al-Jazari's automata, Frankenstein's creature and Čapek's robots belong to the "prehistory" of AI, or to another history: the history of our fears and desires?
  • Were the AI "winters" scientific failures, or failures to keep promises?
  • Who writes the history of AI? Which regions, languages and figures are most often missing from it?
Roles
A moderator; two "historians" who present the scientific timeline; a "reader" who defends the importance of fiction; a rapporteur.
Preparation
Reread the historical overview in chapter 1. Each participant brings a date, a document and a question.
Reading suggestions
A. M. Turing, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence", Mind, 1950; J.-G. Ganascia, L'IA expliquée aux humains, Seuil, 2024; M. Shelley, Frankenstein, 1818; K. Čapek, R.U.R., 1920.

The impact of AI on thinking and methods in the humanities and social sciences (SHS)

Counting words, grouping novels, measuring the sentiment of a corpus: these operations change the way questions are asked in literature and in the social sciences. The distant reading proposed by Franco Moretti has attracted as much enthusiasm as criticism. This round table examines what AI does to method, to evidence and to the work of the researcher.

Questions for the debate

  • Does distant reading complement close reading, or does it risk pushing it to the margins?
  • Is a result produced by a model (a grouping of novels, a sentiment score) a proof, a clue or a hypothesis to be discussed?
  • What skills must a researcher in comparative literature now acquire, and which ones should they never hand over to a machine?
  • How can an AI-assisted analysis be made verifiable and reproducible by another researcher?
Roles
A moderator; a "close reading" team and a "distant reading" team, with two people each; a rapporteur.
Preparation
Each team chooses a concrete corpus (a collection, a set of speeches, the novels of one decade). It shows what its method reveals in this corpus, and what it misses.
Reading suggestions
F. Moretti, Distant Reading, Verso, 2013; J. Robert, "Natural Language Processing (NLP): définition et principes", DataScientest, 2024; B. Prieur, Traitement automatique du langage naturel avec Python; K. Baddari and A. Herzallah, La recherche de l'information, OPU, 2015.

AI as a source of new scientific and social issues

AI does not just answer old questions: it creates new ones. Data bias, responsibility when something goes wrong, the place of low-resource languages, the status of the author and of plagiarism, the protection of personal data: these problems concern both research and society. Comparative surveys of values, such as the World Values Survey, also remind us that not all societies share the same norms.

Questions for the debate

  • Large models are trained mostly on texts in English. What are the consequences for Arabic, Darja, Tamazight and the cultures they carry?
  • If values vary widely from one society to another, can a chatbot claim to be neutral? Which values does it encode?
  • Who is responsible when an AI system makes a serious error: the designer, the company, the user, the state?
  • Does generative AI transform the notions of author, originality and plagiarism, or does it only force us to define them more precisely?
Roles
A moderator; three or four speakers who each speak for one stakeholder (a researcher, a lawyer, a teacher, a social media user); a rapporteur.
Preparation
Each speaker presents a real, documented case that illustrates their point of view, with its source.
Reading suggestions
R. Inglehart, Modernization and Postmodernization: Cultural, Economic, and Political Change in 43 Societies, Princeton University Press, 1997; "Western values are diverging from the rest of the world's", The Economist, 2023; L. Langlois, M.-A. Dilhac, J. Dratwa et al., L'éthique au cœur de l'IA, Obvia, 2023; UNESCO, Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence, 2021.

The outlook for AI

Between claims that general AI is about to arrive and outright rejection, it is hard to form a judgment. This round table looks ten years ahead: careers in literature and languages, the university, regulation, the place of the countries of the Global South. It must end with a concrete proposal rather than a prophecy.

Questions for the debate

  • Which careers in literature and languages (translation, publishing, teaching, journalism) will AI transform, and which will resist best?
  • What criteria should we use to judge claims that general AI, or a technological "singularity", is near?
  • What place can Algeria have in producing AI tools, and not only in using them: corpora, models, training, regulation?
  • What rules on the use of AI should your department adopt for master's theses?
Roles
A moderator; an "optimistic foresight" group and a "critical foresight" group; a rapporteur who drafts, together with the class, a short charter on AI use.
Preparation
Reread the "Outlook" lesson in chapter 1. Each group puts forward three reasoned proposals for the charter.
Reading suggestions
J.-G. Ganascia, L'IA expliquée aux humains, Seuil, 2024; J.-G. Ganascia, Le Mythe de la Singularité, Seuil, 2017; Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 on artificial intelligence; UNESCO, Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence, 2021.

Preparing and running a round table

A good round table is not a series of presentations. It is an exchange in which everyone listens, responds and adds nuance. The following procedure will help you get there.

  1. Form the group and share out the roles

    One to two weeks before the session, form a group of five or six people and choose the moderator, the speakers and the rapporteur. Everyone knows what is expected of them.

  2. Define the question

    Choose two of the four questions suggested and turn them into one clear research question, stated in a single sentence.

  3. Do the reading

    Each speaker reads at least one of the references listed and notes two arguments and one concrete example, with their exact source. Any information obtained from an AI tool is checked against an independent source.

  4. Prepare your contribution

    Prepare an opening statement of three minutes at most, on a card with key ideas, not a text to read aloud. Anticipate one objection and the answer you would give.

  5. Run the debate

    The moderator introduces the question and the participants, gives each person the floor, keeps the discussion going, watches the time and opens the discussion to the class for the last fifteen minutes.

  6. Report back

    The rapporteur writes a one-page summary: points of agreement, disagreements, questions that remain open, and references cited. It is shared with the whole class.

Checklist before and after the session

  • The question under debate fits in one clear sentence.
  • Everyone knows the roles before the session.
  • Each speaker relies on at least one identified source.
  • The examples come from real corpora or real situations.
  • Opposing positions have been presented fairly.
  • Speaking time has been shared evenly among the participants.
  • Any use of an AI tool during preparation is declared.
  • The summary separates agreements, disagreements and open questions.
