One subject or three? Check the instructions

The official syllabus gives two different instructions. In the presentation of the mini-project, it says: "Work on 1 subject of your choice from" the five subjects. In the "Teaching method" part, it says: "Mini-project (complete at least 3 of the 5 proposed subjects)".

Check with your teacher which rule applies before you start. If you must work on several subjects, adapt the schedule below together with your teacher.

Rules for all subjects

  • Sources. For each dataset, note who produced it, where you found it, on what date and how you collected it.
  • Privacy. Remove people's names, usernames and identifiers. In Algeria, Law No. 18-07 of 10 June 2018 governs the processing of personal data. For a questionnaire, ask the respondents for their consent and keep them anonymous.
  • Rights. Respect the platforms' terms of use and copyright: you may analyze a recent novel, but you may not republish it online.
  • Reproducibility. Another person must be able to rerun your notebook and get the same results.
  • Transparency. If you use a generative AI assistant to write code or rephrase a text, say so in the report and check everything it produces.

Deliverables and schedule

Whatever the subject, you must produce three things. The exact arrangements (due date, length of the oral presentation, how to submit) are set by your teacher.

The notebook

A Jupyter notebook is a document that mixes code cells, results and explanatory text. Yours must run without errors from start to finish after a kernel restart. Include the data, or explain exactly how to obtain it.

The report (4 to 6 pages)

Suggested outline: research question, data, method, results, interpretation, limitations and ethical issues, references. Charts are numbered, given a title and discussed in the text.

The oral presentation

A short presentation: your question, your main result, one limitation of your approach. Be ready to show a code cell and explain what it does.

Suggested three-week schedule

  1. Week 1 · Choose and define your scope

    Choose the subject, write a precise research question and one or two hypotheses. Ask your teacher to approve this framework.

  2. Week 1 · Gather the data

    Collect the corpus or the answers, write a source sheet, anonymize, then load everything into a pandas table.

  3. Week 2 · Explore and clean

    Count, sort, find duplicates and missing values, and make a few descriptive charts. Write down each cleaning decision.

  4. Week 2 · Apply the method

    Use the tool or algorithm of your subject. Check the results on a small sample that you read or check yourself.

  5. Week 3 · Interpret and discuss

    Go back to the texts or the data to explain what the figures show. Identify biases and limitations.

  6. Week 3 · Write and present

    Write the report, rerun the whole notebook, prepare the oral presentation.

Subject 1. Sentiment analysis on social media

Sentiment analysis (analyse de sentiments) gives a text a polarity: positive, negative or neutral, sometimes with an intensity score. The simplest tools rely on a lexicon of scored words; the most recent ones are language models trained on annotated texts.

Objective
Analyze text corpora from social networks (Twitter, Facebook, etc.) to determine users' sentiments about a particular topic, for example a political campaign, a health crisis or a social event.
Possible research question
How did internet users receive the film or TV adaptation of a novel? Does the tone of the comments change between the announcement of the adaptation and its release, and does it depend on the language of the comments?
Data
Read access to the X (formerly Twitter) API became paid in 2023. This strongly limits the use of the Tweepy library for student work. Prefer existing public corpora: Sentiment140 (tweets in English), the Allociné corpus (film reviews in French, available on the Hugging Face Hub), LABR (readers' book reviews in Arabic), or public datasets from a platform such as Kaggle. You can also collect a few hundred public comments by hand (under YouTube videos, online press articles, or readers' reviews). Respect the site's terms of use and delete the usernames.
Deliverables
The anonymized corpus (CSV file with the source and date of each text), the notebook, a hand-annotated sample to check the tool, charts (distribution of sentiments, change over time, possibly a map), the report.

Tools listed in the syllabus

  • Python
  • TextBlob
  • VADER
  • Tweepy (for Twitter)
  • pandas
  • matplotlib
  • GeoPandas

GeoPandas is used to draw maps. It is only useful if your texts are linked to a place (wilaya, country), which is rare in public data.

Approach

  1. Define the topic

    Set the object (a work, an event), the period and the language or languages you study.

  2. Build the corpus

    Gather the texts in a table with at least the columns texte, date, langue and source.

  3. Clean

    Remove duplicates, links and empty messages. Keep the emoticons: VADER takes them into account.

  4. Annotate a sample by hand

    Read about fifty texts and note their polarity yourself. This sample will be your reference.

  5. Apply the tool and compare

    Compute the scores, then count, on the sample, the cases where the tool agrees with your reading.

  6. Visualize

    Chart of the distribution of sentiments, curve of the change week by week, map with GeoPandas if a place is known.

  7. Interpret

    Reread the most positive and the most negative texts, and look for the tool's typical errors (irony, negation, dialect).

Installation: pip install vaderSentiment pandas matplotlib. Run the program, then compare each score with your own reading: for you, is a comment such as "nothing special" neutral or negative?

What about texts in French or Arabic?

VADER's lexicon is in English. On French text, it recognizes almost no words and returns scores close to zero. Here are three solutions to test on your annotated sample:

  • a multilingual model from the transformers library, for example cardiffnlp/twitter-xlm-roberta-base-sentiment, trained on tweets in several languages including Arabic, French and English (check the list of languages on the model card);
  • TextBlob with the textblob-fr extension, which adds an analyzer for French; it is an old extension, so check that it works with your version of Python;
  • machine translation into English before VADER, with a note in the report that translation can change the tone.

None of these tools deals well with darja written in Latin script ("arabizi") or with irony: plan a manual check.

Link to comparative literature

Sentiment analysis can become a tool for studying reception. Compare readers' reviews of the same novel read in its original language and in translation: do Arabic-, French- and English-speaking readers value the same aspects of the work? This connects with the notion of the "horizon of expectations" proposed by Hans Robert Jauss. Keep in mind that a score measures the color of the words used, not the quality of a critical judgment.

Subject 2. Clustering social behavior in surveys

Clustering (clustering, partitionnement) groups together individuals who resemble each other, without being given categories in advance: it is unsupervised learning. The k-means (k-moyennes) algorithm looks for k groups in which each member is close to the center of its group.

Objective
Use clustering techniques to group individuals with similar social behaviors based on survey data. This can be useful for segmenting a population into distinct interest groups.
Possible research question
Are there distinct reader profiles among Master's students: reading languages (Arabic, French, English), medium (paper, screen), frequency, favorite genres?
Data
A questionnaire that you design yourself (closed questions, scales from 1 to 5 or from 1 to 10), anonymous and filled in with the respondents' consent. Or data from international surveys: the World Values Survey, cited in the course references (overview by Western Michigan University, data on the official website after you accept the terms of use), or the Arab Barometer, which surveyed Algerians in several waves. Work on one country and about ten chosen variables.
Deliverables
The variable dictionary (question asked, coding of the answers), the notebook, the justification for the number of groups, a table of average profiles, charts, the report.

Tools listed in the syllabus

  • Python
  • pandas
  • sklearn
  • matplotlib
  • seaborn
  • scikit-learn

The syllabus lists both sklearn and scikit-learn: they are the same library. You install it under the name scikit-learn and import it under the name sklearn.

Approach

  1. Choose and code the variables

    Turn each answer into a number (scales) or into 0/1 columns for multiple-choice answers (pd.get_dummies).

  2. Handle missing answers

    Remove questionnaires that are too incomplete, or replace a missing value with the median, and say so.

  3. Standardize

    Put the variables on the same scale. Otherwise, the variable with the largest numbers dominates the distance calculation.

  4. Choose the number of groups

    Try several values of k and compare them with the elbow method and the silhouette score (silhouette_score).

  5. Run k-means

    Assign a group to each respondent.

  6. Describe the profiles

    Compute the mean of each variable per group, then name the groups with caution.

  7. Test the stability

    Rerun with another random_state or another k: the main profiles should remain recognizable.

The group numbers (0, 1, 2) mean nothing in themselves: only the table of means lets you describe each profile.

Link to the humanities and social sciences (SHS) and literature

Using the World Values Survey, Ronald Inglehart, whose 1997 book appears in the course references, and Christian Welzel proposed a "cultural map" that places societies on two axes: traditional or secular-rational values, and survival or self-expression values. Your clustering also produces a typology. Like Max Weber's ideal types, its groups are constructions: they simplify reality to make it readable. Applied to the reading practices of multilingual readers, it can shed light on the place of each language in the literary life of students.

Subject 3. Text analysis for information extraction from political speeches

A named entity (entité nommée) is an expression that refers to a specific being or object: a person, a place, an organization, a date. Topic modeling (modélisation thématique) finds groups of words that often appear together in a corpus. Its best-known method, LDA, was proposed by David Blei, Andrew Ng and Michael Jordan in 2003.

Objective
Use AI to extract the main themes and named entities (names of people, places, dates) from political speeches. This can be used to understand politicians' concerns and to analyze their rhetoric.
Possible research question
Which themes and which places dominate the speeches given by heads of state during the general debate of the United Nations General Assembly? Do they vary by region or by the original language of the speech?
Data
Public transcripts of speeches: official websites of presidencies and governments, the vie-publique.fr website (a collection of French public speeches), or the UN General Debate Corpus, which brings together the speeches of the UN general debate since 1970 (in English). For each speech, note the speaker, the date, the place and the language, and state whether it is the original or a translation.
Deliverables
The corpus with its metadata, the notebook, a table of the most frequent entities, the list of topics with your interpretations, word clouds and charts, the report.

Tools listed in the syllabus

  • Python
  • spaCy
  • Transformers (BERT)
  • gensim (LDA)
  • matplotlib
  • WordCloud

Approach

  1. Build the corpus

    One file per speech, or a table with one row per speech and its metadata.

  2. Clean

    Remove notes that are not part of the speech ("applause", headers, page numbers).

  3. Preprocess with spaCy

    Split the text into words, reduce each word to its lemma (its dictionary form), remove stop words (articles, prepositions).

  4. Extract and count the entities

    People, places, organizations; count them per speech and per speaker.

  5. Model the topics

    Try several numbers of topics and keep the one whose word lists are easiest to interpret. Read the most representative speeches for each topic.

  6. Visualize

    Frequency charts, word clouds by speaker or by period.

  7. Analyze the rhetoric

    Go back to the passages: which phrases, which places, which dates does the speaker use, and for what effect?

Of course, four sentences are not enough for LDA: this skeleton shows how the steps follow one another. On a real corpus, plan for several dozen speeches at the very least.

Limitations of the available models

  • spaCy's French models (fr_core_news_sm, fr_core_news_md…) recognize four types of entities: persons (PER), places (LOC), organizations (ORG) and miscellaneous (MISC). They do not detect dates. Use regular expressions (the re module) or a BERT-type model fine-tuned for entity recognition. Choose it on the Hugging Face Hub, and check which languages and entity types it covers.
  • spaCy does not offer an official trained model for Arabic. For speeches in Arabic, look for dedicated tools (for example CAMeL Tools) or Arabic BERT models, and check them on a sample.

Link to literature

The same tools apply to novels. If you extract all the places mentioned in a corpus of novels, you can draw a literary geography, in the spirit of Franco Moretti's Atlas of the European Novel 1800-1900. Compare, for example, the places in Algerian novels written in French and in Arabic. Finally, note that the English word topic comes from the Greek topos, the "place" of classical rhetoric: in its own way, topic modeling rediscovers the commonplaces of a discourse.

Subject 4. Predictive modeling of social behavior from consumption data

A predictive model learns from examples whose outcome is known, in order to estimate the outcome of new cases: this is supervised learning. Logistic regression (régression logistique), despite its name, is used to classify: it computes a probability between 0 and 1, for example the probability that a person attends an event.

Objective
Build a predictive model to estimate consumers' social behaviors (for example the probability of buying products or of taking part in social events) based on various variables.
Possible research question
Which factors (age, reading practices, distance, cost, language of the event) are associated with students' attendance at cultural events: book fair, literary café, meeting with an author?
Data
A questionnaire that you design, with a final question such as "Did you attend a literary event this year? (yes/no)". With only a few dozen answers, be very careful in your conclusions. To practice on larger data, the UCI Machine Learning Repository offers public datasets on consumer behavior, such as "Online Shoppers Purchasing Intention" (whether or not a visit to an online store ends in a purchase) or "Bank Marketing" (whether or not a customer subscribes to a bank offer).
Deliverables
The questionnaire or the description of the public dataset, the notebook, the comparison with a naive baseline model, the evaluation metrics, the interpretation of the coefficients, the report.

Tools listed in the syllabus

  • Python
  • sklearn
  • pandas
  • matplotlib
  • seaborn

Approach

  1. Define the variable to predict

    A column coded 1 (attended, bought) or 0 (did not).

  2. Choose the explanatory variables

    Code them as numbers; turn categories into 0/1 columns.

  3. Explore

    With seaborn, compare the distributions of the variables for participants and non-participants.

  4. Split training and test data

    The model learns on some of the rows and is evaluated on the others, which it has never seen.

  5. Train and compare

    Compare logistic regression with a naive model that always predicts the most frequent answer: a good model must do better.

  6. Evaluate

    Accuracy (exactitude) is the share of correct predictions; precision is the share of real participants among those that the model predicts as participants; recall (rappel) is the share of real participants that the model finds.

  7. Interpret and discuss

    The sign of the coefficients (modele.coef_) shows the direction of the association, not a cause. Discuss the biases of the sample.

Here, the test set contains only four people and the fictional data are very regular: a high score would prove nothing. On your own data, look above all at the confusion matrix (confusion_matrix) and at the comparison with the naive model.

Link to the sociology of culture and to literature

Pierre Bourdieu, in La Distinction (1979), showed that cultural practices are linked to educational capital and social background: these variables belong in your model, if you collect them with the respondents' agreement. On the literary side, researchers have tried to predict the success of novels from their textual features, such as Jodie Archer and Matthew Jockers in The Bestseller Code (2016). This work is debated: predicting is not explaining, and a model trained on past successes tends to reproduce the tastes of the past.

Subject 5. Social network analysis (sociology of networks)

A graph represents relationships: nodes (people, characters) connected by links or edges (friendship, exchange of letters, presence in the same scene). Social network analysis (analyse des réseaux sociaux, SNA) measures the position of each member in this network. Degree centrality gives the share of the other nodes to which a node is directly connected; betweenness centrality (centralité d'intermédiarité) shows how often a node lies on the shortest paths between the others, in other words how much it acts as a bridge.

Objective
Use social network analysis (SNA) techniques to study the relationships between individuals in a social network, using structured data.
Possible research question
Can we see the fragmented structure of Nedjma (Kateb Yacine, 1956) in the network of its characters? Who connects the groups, who stays on the margins, and does the network change from one part of the novel to another?
Data
Structured data, that is, a table of links (source, target, weight). You can build it by hand, noting section by section which characters appear together; or from an anonymous survey on the relationships in a group (a sociogram), with the participants' consent. For practice, NetworkX contains classic networks: nx.les_miserables_graph() (co-appearances of the characters in Les Misérables), nx.florentine_families_graph() (marriages between Florentine families of the Renaissance) and nx.karate_club_graph() (the karate club studied by Wayne Zachary in 1977). Nedjma is protected by copyright: share your link tables, not the digitized text.
Deliverables
The link table (CSV) with the rule that defines a link, the notebook, the centrality measures, a Python visualization and a Gephi visualization, the GEXF file, the report.

Tools listed in the syllabus

  • Python
  • NetworkX
  • Gephi
  • matplotlib
  • pandas

Gephi is free software for network visualization. It is installed separately from Python. NetworkX does the calculations; Gephi does the layout.

Approach

  1. Define nodes and links

    Who counts as a character? What is a link: appearing in the same section, talking to each other, writing to each other? Write this rule down: everything else depends on it.

  2. Build the link table

    One row per pair of characters, with a weight (number of shared sections). Save it as a CSV file.

  3. Create the graph

    Load the table with pandas, then nx.from_pandas_edgelist(df, "source", "cible", "poids").

  4. Compute the measures

    Degree, betweenness, and possibly communities with nx.community.louvain_communities.

  5. Visualize

    A first drawing with matplotlib, then a careful layout in Gephi from the GEXF file.

  6. Compare

    Change the link rule, compare two parts of the novel, or compare with another novel.

  7. Interpret

    Compare the figures with your reading: is a character who is central to the network also central to the meaning of the work?

The six characters are really those of the novel, but the links and weights in this script are invented for the example: do not quote them as a result. Add to the list the other characters that you note while reading.

Link to comparative literature

Franco Moretti, in "Network Theory, Plot Analysis" (2011), represented the plot of Hamlet as a network and observed what happens to the play when a character is removed. You can compare the network of Nedjma, a novel with a fragmented chronology, with that of a novel with a linear structure, or with the embedded tales of the One Thousand and One Nights, where the network of narrators reveals the architecture of the book.
